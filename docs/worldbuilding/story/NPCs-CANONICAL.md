**SPACEFACE — CANONICAL NPCs (RECURRING CONTRACT ACTORS)**

Eight names appear on contract boards and after-action logs across sectors. They do not introduce themselves. They do not explain. They transact, log, and leave. The graffiti that follows is written in the same hand that signed the manifest.

**KESSLER** logs cargo at Tycho Relay. "Weight matches prior haul." Thumb twice on the seal. Manifest logged before scan clears. Later: "variance adjustment." Graffiti (first visit): THE SCALE LIES WHEN THE THUMB IS ON IT. Graffiti (second visit, different hand): KESSLER. SCALE 4. TYCHO. TWENTY-TWO MONTHS. LOOK IT UP.

Kessler's been saving the skim for nineteen years to buy his daughter a berth on a Core station where the air is warm. He has the number. The number is precise. The number assumes the skim continues at the current rate for six more years, which it will, because Kessler is the rate. He has never told the daughter. The daughter is nine and thinks her father weighs cargo. The six years will become twelve. The twelve will become twenty. The number will climb and the daughter will grow up breathing Tycho air, which is 18 degrees and yellow-white, and she will marry someone who weighs cargo, and her son will stand at a scale. Kessler knows this is possible. He saves anyway. The savings is the prayer. The prayer is the only honest thing on the scale.

**VOSS** runs the claim recorder at Hollow Station. "Vein looks played out. Move on." Mask down. Cutter finishes. Claim filed exhausted same shift new crew arrives. Prior markers stripped. Graffiti (on the claim post): THE ROCK REMEMBERS WHO FILED FIRST. Graffiti (lower, added after, unattributed): THE SECOND SHIFT FOUND MARKERS. THEY'RE GONE NOW. VOSS WAS ON THAT SHIFT.

Voss files exhausted claims because Voss is building a private map of every shaft the system has sealed and re-sealed, and the map is the only thing Voss trusts. The map is for the day Voss goes back into the sealed shafts alone — after the claims lapse, after the crews stop coming — and works the played-out veins that were never played out, only filed that way. Voss believes there is a fortune in the exhausted columns. There may be. The shafts Voss means to reopen include Shaft 7, which is not exhausted of anything a miner would want. What Shaft 7 has is a sealed hopper, a double-pass weld, and the reason the Pit's air went bad. Voss does not know this. Voss will find out, or Voss will not. The map does not have a column for what is actually in the shafts. The map only has coordinates.

**MIRA** handles sealed freight at Bourse. "Route's clear. Payment on delivery." She swaps the manifest codes at the relay while the comms window stays open on the prior client's channel. The crate arrives with new seals. The original client receives the seizure notice. Graffiti inside the cargo hold: THE SEAL WAS NEVER YOURS. Graffiti at the loading dock (added later, different hand): MIRA NEVER TOUCHES THE CRATE. THAT'S HOW YOU KNOW WHAT'S IN IT.

Mira swaps codes to fund a passage offworld — one ticket, one-way, to a system where no one has heard of Bourse or the Quiet or her name. She has the ticket picked out. She has been two jobs away from it for four years. There are always two more jobs. The ticket is real. The route exists. The thing that keeps her at the relay is not the money, which is enough, but the suspicion that the moment she stops swapping codes, the system will notice she existed. She cannot prove this. The suspicion is the cage. She swaps another code, and the two-jobs-away number holds, and the ticket stays bought but unclaimed.

**ROOK** coordinates bounty tags from a booth at Cinder. "Target's last ping was here. Standard fee." The stylus hovers over the payout line, then adds a second signature below it. The same target ID appears on a second board the next cycle. Rook's cut is already deducted from both. Graffiti on the comms panel: ONE NAME. TWO PAYERS. Below it, cut off: ROOK OWES ME—

Rook sees himself as the sector's real referee — the man who decides, tag by tag, whose problem a body becomes. He keeps a private tally of every double-bill and tells himself the tally is leverage, that one day he'll trade it to Concord for a pardon and a badge and a booth at a Core station where the air is warm. The pardon is a fantasy. Concord does not pardon ledgers; it absorbs them. Rook's tally is not leverage. It is a confession written in installments, and the installment plan never ends, and the booth at Cinder is the last booth Rook will ever sit in. He knows this on the nights he counts the tally. He double-bills again in the morning anyway, because the morning is when the next name comes across the board, and the name has to go somewhere, and Rook's booth is where names go.

The first time Rook handled a double-tag was not for an ore dispute. Rook double-billed a bounty on a Pit maintenance engineer named Elroy who filed an administrative report noting that the primary recycler catalyst grid had been decommissioned without authorization and the components had disappeared. The report was re-classified as "SECTOR INTERFERENCE — PIRATE THREAT" through Rook's booth at Cinder. Two clients paid. One was Vale's intermediary. Elroy is listed in the Kurtz figure's ledger as "COUNTERPARTY — DECEASED (B2)." The "B2" refers to the player's second contract, not a sector designation. Rook doesn't know this connection. Rook tagged Elroy as a standard double-bill. The double-bill was just more convenient than a single payout. That's all it was to Rook.

**SLATE** welds hull patches at the Pit shipyard. "This'll hold till the next gate." The torch cuts the same seam twice, the second pass narrower. The patch fails at the first pressure spike. Slate is already on the next berth quoting replacement cost. Graffiti on the repair bay door: THE WELD KNOWS WHO CUT IT TWICE.

Slate runs a side business in bad welds — not for the skim, but for the *inventory*. Every ship Slate patches twice is a ship Slate knows the failure point of, and Slate keeps a private list of those failure points the way a card counter keeps the deck. The list is Slate's retirement: one day the right ship comes through with the right owner and the right insurance, and Slate's second pass is the thing that turns a repair job into a salvage claim, and the salvage claim is the score that buys the berth Slate has been quoting other people for twenty years. The right ship has not come through. The list grows. The failure points accumulate. Slate has become a man who knows exactly how every hull in the sector will break and who is waiting, with the patience of a man who has nothing else, for the one break that pays.

**DRIFT** records ore deliveries at Meridian Exchange. "Quota met. Credit transferred." The ledger page turns before the scale settles. The shortfall is logged as "moisture loss." Drift's own account shows the difference deposited under a subsidiary code. Graffiti above the scale: THE VEIN PAYS THE MAN WHO WEIGHS IT.

Drift tells himself the moisture-loss entries are the cost of doing business in a system that underpays him, and that the subsidiary code is the pension the exchange never funded. Drift is, in his own estimation, an honest man forced into one dishonesty by an dishonest scale, and the dishonesty is bounded and the books will balance one day when he pays it back. Drift has been paying it back, in his head, for sixteen years. The principal has never decreased. The interest is the thing Drift doesn't call interest, because calling it interest would make him Kessler, and Drift is not Kessler, Drift is a man with a pension the exchange forgot to fund. He will retire on the moisture loss. The moisture loss will retire him. These are the same sentence and Drift has not noticed.

**HALE** inspects for Concord customs at Gate 3. "Scan complete. No flags." The handheld unit records the crate contents before the lid opens. The fine arrives at the next gate under a different regulation number. Hale initials both entries. Graffiti on the scanner housing: THE LOG REMEMBERS WHAT THE EYE SKIPPED. Scratched into the housing below it, smaller: HALE INITIALED BOTH. GATE 3. YEAR 7. THE SECOND ENTRY IS FILED UNDER REG 44-C. GO LOOK.

Hale is not corrupt. Hale runs the scanner. The scanner says what it says. The regulation says what the regulation says. The double fine is not an action Hale takes — it is a provision of REG 44-C that Hale correctly applies. Someone else wrote the regulation. Someone else put the provision in. Hale initials the second entry because the second entry is legally required and Hale has never, in seventeen years, failed to initial a legally required entry. The priest does not look in the hold. The priest administers the sacrament. The hold is someone else's department.

Hale's register when the manifest says one thing and the scan says another: "The manifest says titanium. The scan says 12,400 kilograms. The seal is Concord ALA. Under REG 44-C, I don't break Concord seals. If the Pit is short of Silt this cycle, they can file an appeal with Logistics Oversight. I don't change the numbers on the sheet. I just sign it." *(Hale is describing the recycler catalyst grid that the player is moving in B0. Hale doesn't know it's a recycler catalyst grid. Hale doesn't break Concord seals. The "Silt" appeal goes to Vale's division. Vale's division processes reversions. This is not a breakdown in the system. This is the system.)*

**QUINN** changes credits at the bar on Outpost 9. "Rate's posted. No questions." The stack is counted once under the bar light, then again under the UV. The short count is replaced with house scrip before the customer looks down. Quinn wipes the counter with the same rag. Graffiti on the till drawer: THE COUNT ENDS WHEN THE DRAWER CLOSES. On the underside of the bar, visible only if you're sitting: QUINN KEEPS THE CHANGE.

Quinn is not skimming to get by. Quinn is three years, by his own arithmetic, from buying the bar. Then the station. Then his name off every Pit hierarchy that ever held it. He keeps a second ledger — not the house ledger, *his* ledger — in a compartment under the till, and every short count goes into it, and the number climbs, and the climb is the only thing in Quinn's life that has ever gone the direction he pointed it. He thinks the player is a mark in his long game: another tired pilot who'll change credits here twice a cycle for a year and a half and never once look under the bar. The player probably is. Quinn is wrong about what game they're both in. The skim that funds Quinn's exit is the same skim that feeds the eight-node runoff that keeps the Pit's air Chalk, and Quinn doesn't know that, and would not care if told, because Quinn's three-year plan does not include a column for forty thousand people he's never met. He is a node who thinks he's the protagonist. The pity and the laugh live in the same place: the second ledger is real, the arithmetic is sound, and the exit it's buying him does not exist. The station isn't for sale. The hierarchy doesn't sell names back. Quinn will die behind that bar with the second ledger two-thirds full and the number still climbing, and the number will be the most honest thing in the room.

Quinn's scheme is also the one that backfired upward into a windfall nobody planned. Six years ago the short count on a Vael courier's stack triggered the courier's pattern-match alert — the Vael addendum flags short counts, page 9 again — and the courier's outfit retaliated by pulling their entire currency business from Outpost 9 and routing it through Meridian. The loss cleaned Quinn out for a cycle. But the Vael pullout left a vacuum, and MTS filled it within the quarter at worse rates, and the worse rates made Outpost 9's currency desk *more* profitable for the station's holding company than the Vael business had ever been. The holding company credited the gain to "market repositioning" and promoted the station manager. Quinn got nothing. Quinn's skim created a windfall that made the people above him richer and the people around him poorer (the Vael rates had subsidized the commissary; the MTS rates don't). The windfall appears in the Kurtz figure's ledger under Outpost 9, year 11, annotated in the margin with a single word: *unintended*. Quinn still doesn't know he caused it. He thinks he's three years from buying the bar.

Eight names. Contract boards. After-action logs. They transact. They log. They leave. Graffiti appears one cycle after. Station maintenance covers it. New lines appear same locations next visit.
