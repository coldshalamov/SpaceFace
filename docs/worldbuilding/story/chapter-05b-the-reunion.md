## THE BOOTH (before the reunion)

Meridian Exchange is the cleanest station the player has docked at. The air is full-spectrum. The lighting does not flicker. The dockmaster looked up when the player paid the berth fee. These are disorienting. The player has been breathing degraded air for long enough that full-spectrum light reads as interrogation and a dockmaster who looks up reads as surveillance. The exchange floor is open and bright and full of people whose clothes are clean and whose hands do not shake and whose ledgers balance on the first pass. The player is the dirtiest object on the floor. The player knows this. The floor knows this. Nobody says anything, because saying it would require acknowledging that the exchange has a floor and the floor has a texture and the texture is "people who can afford to be here" and the player is not, by that texture, one of them.

Callum's booth is mid-floor. Not the best location — the best locations are held by institutions. Callum's booth is the best location held by a person. The booth has a screen showing commodity futures. The booth has a chair. The booth has Callum, in clean clothes, trading. The booth does not have a back room. Callum does not need a back room. Everything Callum does is legal. Everything Callum did is filed.

The player walks the floor. The player is the only person on the floor who has killed someone this week. The player knows this. The floor does not. The floor sees a pilot in a worn suit with a transponder that's been through too many owners. The floor is not wrong. The floor is incomplete.

## THE RECOGNITION

Callum does not recognize Wren immediately. This is not a performance. It has been nine years. Wren spent four of them frozen. The man who walks up to the booth is thirty-one, looks thirty-five, and carries himself like a man who learned to move quietly in a place where sound meant detection. Callum is looking at futures. Callum looks up the way a broker looks up — to see if the person at the booth is a client or a waste of time. Callum sees a pilot. The assessment takes half a second. The assessment is: not a client.

Then Callum sees who the pilot is. The recognition is not dramatic. The recognition is the broker's face recalculating. The assessment changes. The new assessment is: a problem that is cheaper to solve with a data chip than with a scene.

```
Wren:    "Callum."
Callum:  "Wren. You look different."
Wren:    "I was frozen for four years."
Callum:  "I heard something about that."
```

The exchange is not warm. The exchange is not cold. The exchange is two men establishing, in three lines, that they both know the history and neither is going to name it on a floor where the exchange can hear. "I heard something about that" is Callum's entire concession. Callum heard Wren was arrested. Callum heard Wren went to a prison ship. Callum heard Wren was frozen. Callum heard all of this and did nothing, because nothing was what the situation called for, and Callum is a man who reliably identifies what a situation calls for and provides exactly that and no more.

## THE QUESTION AND THE VENUE

```
Wren:    "Who'd you sell it to?"
Callum:  "That's not a conversation for the floor."
Wren:    "I'm not on the floor. I'm at your booth."
Callum:  "You're at my booth with a question I'm not going to answer where the exchange can hear it."
Wren:    "The exchange can hear whatever it wants. Who bought the fragment?"
Callum:  "A man who paid well and didn't give a name. That's the whole story."
Wren:    "That's not the whole story. That's the part you're willing to tell while the exchange can hear it."
Callum:  "That's the same thing, Wren."
```

The exchange can hear whatever it wants. This is the load-bearing line of the conversation, and it is the line Callum does not hear himself saying. The exchange CAN hear whatever it wants. The exchange is a market. Markets hear transactions. The conversation is a transaction. The exchange is hearing it. The exchange will hear the data chip change hands. The exchange will hear Callum's booth's ledger record the chip as a courtesy. The exchange will hear Wren leave. The exchange hears everything, because hearing everything is what an exchange is for, and Callum has arranged his entire life around the principle that the exchange hearing things is fine as long as the things the exchange hears are the things Callum is willing to have heard.

"That's the same thing, Wren" is the lie spoken aloud. The part Callum is willing to tell is not the whole story. The part Callum is willing to tell is the part that costs Callum nothing. The whole story costs Callum something — not the fragment, not the buyer's name (Callum may not have the name; Callum may have the name and be protecting the asset; the game does not resolve which, because resolving it would give Callum a legibility he has spent nine years avoiding). The whole story costs Callum the admission that the transaction he describes as resolved was never resolved, that the chip is not closure but a payoff, and that Callum's prosperity is built on the specific skill of describing payoffs as resolutions in a voice that sounds complete.

## THE CHIP

Callum slides a data chip across the counter. The chip is small. The chip is a Concord evidence-transfer format. The chip contains a partial transaction record — enough to trace the buyer to a facility, not enough to identify the buyer. The facility is in Concord administrative space. The facility's designation appears in the REF 44-C system under "EVIDENCE PROCESSING — LONG-TERM STORAGE."

Callum does not slide the chip across the counter out of guilt. Callum slides the chip across the counter because Wren at his booth is bad for business. A pilot in a worn suit standing at a broker's booth asking questions the exchange can hear is the kind of thing that makes the other brokers look. The looking is the cost. The chip is cheaper than the looking. Callum has done the arithmetic. The arithmetic is: chip < scene. The chip changes hands. The scene does not happen. The arithmetic is correct.

```
Callum:  "That traces the buyer to a facility. You can follow it from there.
          We're done."
Wren:    "We're not done."
Callum:  "We're done for the floor. Anything else is a different conversation
          in a different room, and I don't have a different room, because I
          don't need one. I trade futures. I don't trade fragments. That was
          nine years ago. The chip is today. Take the chip."
```

## THE TWO READINGS OF THE CHIP (civilian / operation)

**Civilian reading:** the chip is the next link in Wren's personal search. Callum sold the first fragment to a buyer who put it back into evidence storage under REF 44-C. The chip traces the buyer to the facility. The chain of custody is a circle — stolen from evidence, sold to a broker, returned to evidence. The chip gives Wren the facility. The facility gives Wren the file. The file gives Wren the trail. The trail leads, eventually, to the coordinates that might lead home. This is the reading Wren carries. This is the reading the player carries on first playthrough. It is true.

**Operation reading (Le Carré layer):** the "man who paid well and didn't give a name" was Brandt, operating through the Quiet. The first fragment was bought by the Quiet because Brandt needed the evidence out of Concord's hands and into the only channel Concord couldn't audit. the mole network has run fourteen years; Callum has been an unwitting node for nine. The chip's trail leads to the same REF 44-C facility because the facility IS the channel Aldiss uses — Aldiss's file-status leaks route through the facility's evidence-transfer logs, encoded in the ATMO TOKEN brokerage's transfer metadata. The chip is not just the next link in Wren's personal search. The chip is the thread the operation has been waiting for Wren to pull, because Wren pulling it in civilian cover is the move that exposes the channel without the operation having to expose itself. Callum thinks the chip resolves Wren's problem. The chip resolves the operation's problem. The two resolutions are filed under the same transaction. Callum is the Le Carré innocent — the civilian whose honest work is the adversary's channel, and who will never know, because the channel depends on the civilian not knowing.

Both readings are true. The operation depends on both being true. The player who reaches B7 and reads the ledger can come back to this chip and read it differently. The player who doesn't has the first reading intact.

## THE UNPUNISHED MAN (the structural mirror to B2)

At B2, the player killed a civilian and was punished with a cleared payment. At B5, the player meets the man who betrayed him and finds the betrayer unpunished and prospering. The system filed Callum's betrayal as a business decision. The system filed Wren's arrest as a flag in a sweep. The two filings are both correct. Neither is just. The system does not deal in justice. The system deals in filings.

The man who sold Wren out for a share and a fragment is sitting at a booth in clean clothes with a good reputation, and the worst consequence he suffered was the mild inconvenience of Wren appearing at his booth nine years later. The inconvenience is being managed with a data chip. The inconvenience will be gone in ten minutes. The chip is cheaper than the scene. The arithmetic is correct. The arithmetic is the wound.

The player who understands this is the player who has begun to understand the game. The reconciliation is not coming. The betrayer stays prosperous. The data chip is the only thing that changes hands. The player leaves Meridian with a lead and no satisfaction. The game does not bring Callum back for closure. Callum's filing is BUSINESS DECISION — RESOLVED. The filing is correct. The filing is the wound. Callum's filing is BUSINESS DECISION — RESOLVED. The filing is correct. The filing is the wound.

## THE EXIT

Wren takes the chip. Wren does not hit Callum. Wren does not threaten Callum. Wren turns and walks the floor and the floor swallows him the way floors swallow people — without comment, without memory, without a column for the man who just stood at a broker's booth and received a payoff he is calling a lead. The booth is quiet. Callum goes back to futures. The futures are commodities. The commodities are real. The chip is in Wren's pocket. The chip is real. The lead is real. The man who sold his partner is clean-clothed and prosperous and trading futures in a market that will never penalize him for any of it. The market is honest. The clothes are clean. The booth is open.

The HUD, as Wren undocks, prints the manifest. The manifest includes the chip. The manifest does not include a column for satisfaction. The manifest does not include a column for justice. The manifest includes a column for CARGO and a column for MASS and the chip is 0.01t and the mass is correct and the filing is clean and the filing is the wound.
