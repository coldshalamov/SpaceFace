# THE STORYLINE

*The campaign as a novel. Thread A happens to everyone; Thread B happens to Wren; the side
stories cross through both as interludes. This document is the voice standard: every chapter,
cutscene, and runtime string downstream of it should be able to sit inside it without a seam.*

*Canon rulings R1–R15 apply. Protected lines are transcribed exactly. Numbers are law.*

---

## PROLOGUE — THE MANIFEST

```
MANIFEST — TESSERA / VHL-4471-T
SLOT01   TITANIUM ALLOY                     12400KG
SLOT99   UNCLASSIFIED COMPOSITE                3.1 KG   [PERSONAL EFFECTS — DO NOT TRANSFER]
```

The first line was pre-loaded. The second line was planted. The ship's operator had read the
manifest four hundred times and had stopped seeing the second line six months after it appeared,
the way you stop seeing a scar. The manifest did not care what the operator saw. The manifest is
the part of the ship that tells the truth.

It weighed 3.1 kilograms.

## BOOK ZERO — THE COLD START

The crate was already aboard when Wren got there.

That was the first thing, and he let it pass, because a courier lets things pass. The dock log
said the crate had been loaded six hours before he stepped into the cockpit. The seal was
Concord ALA. The mass was twelve point four tonnes, titanium alloy, consigned Helios Prime to
the Pit, and the pay was standard, and standard pay at 04:00 ship-time is the whole story a
courier asks for.

On the dock below, a sweeper named Sump was pushing chalk dust from one side of the bay to the
other, which was the job, and would be the job again tomorrow. He waved at the freighter. He
waved at all the freighters. Nobody had waved back in eleven years and this had never once
occurred to him as a problem.

The departure acknowledgment carried an authorization: `VALE, D. / MID-SECTOR ADMIN / REF 44-C`,
and under it a co-author field, redacted.

The run was quiet until it wasn't. The killfeed told it the way the killfeed tells everything:
`ESCORT-02 ELIMINATED. ESCORT-01 ELIMINATED. CARGO DRONE 03 LOST. UNKNOWN VESSEL DEPARTED.`
Someone had wanted the alloy. Someone had taken exactly the alloy. Someone had known the mass
to the kilogram.

At the Pit, the manifest read `SLOT01 TITANIUM ALLOY 00000KG`. The contract closed itself.
The payment withheld itself. On the inside of the airlock door, in the stencil font the station
used for intake numbers, someone had written:

**THEY KNEW THE MASS.**

Sump was still sweeping when Wren came through. The chalk dust had migrated back to the side
he'd started from, which was also the job.

## INTERLUDE — THE UNKNOWN VESSEL

From a hull-spotter's log, Sector 4, later:

```
SPOTTER LOG / S4 / CYCLE 17
UNKNOWN HULL. NO REGISTRY. NO TRANSponder ECHO.
DEPARTED THE 47-A INTERCEPT WINDOW FOUR MINUTES AFTER CARGO DRONE 03.
IT DID NOT RUN. IT LEFT.
```

Whoever it was had not been hunting the freighter. They had been confirming the bait was loaded.

## BOOK ONE — HONEST WORK

The next contract was already on the board before the airlock finished cycling. The board does
not wait. The board never waits.

Routine alloy run, Tycho. Twelve tonnes this time. Not twelve point four. The category was
INDUSTRIAL COMPONENTS when Wren accepted it and SURPLUS REDISTRIBUTION — STANDARD when he
delivered it, and the change happened mid-transit, and the HUD did not notify him, because the
HUD has never notified anyone, because this is normal, this is what manifests do. The customs
system at Tycho read the current category. The prior category did not happen, in the sense the
system uses the word.

Payment: 340 credits. Correct to the credit. The cargo arrived.

At the Tycho scale, the weigher worked the way a man works when the ritual is the only part of
the job that is his. Kessler. Scale 4. Nineteen years. His thumb went onto the seal twice before
the scan cleared — not because the scan needed it, but because the scale lied differently when
his thumb was on it, and he had decided, somewhere in year two, that if the scale was going to
lie it was going to lie about less. The manifest logged before the scan cleared. Later there
would be a variance adjustment. There was always a variance adjustment later.

The player who talked to the man at the Meridian end of the route got Drift, and Drift was not
waiting to be talked to.

"You want to know about moisture loss. Everybody wants to know about moisture loss. Rock sweats.
I've seen it sweat. I've logged two-tenths light on a Bourse run and initialed it and the column
balances and I go home. That's sixteen years of shifts. One entry in the column is not mine.
Year 3. Different hand. Same moisture. The moisture goes where the moisture goes — into the air,
into the system, into the fund that pays the recyclers that don't work on the decks where the
moisture came from. That's not a joke. I don't tell jokes. The column balances. The column will
balance Thursday."

On the airlock at Tycho, in stencil, in a hand that knew things the player hadn't done yet:

**REDISTRIBUTED TO THE HIGHEST BIDDER.**

## INTERLUDE — THE HAND

Pit maintenance, third shift. Ivo paints over the graffiti at 03:00. The wall is back by 06:00.
His shift log, four years of entries, reads the same on every page:

`BULKHEAD 4. PAINT. WALL WAS CLEAN AT START OF SHIFT.`

He never writes the second sentence. The second sentence would be: *the wall was not clean at
the end of it.* He has never written it. Nobody has asked him to write it. Whether Ivo writes
the walls' other sentences — the ones in stencil — is a question the game does not answer,
because the shop floor does not answer it, and the shop floor has opinions about everything and
evidence about nothing.

The paint is a filing. The wall is a record. Maintenance is the system's way of pretending the
record and the filing agree.

## BOOK TWO — FIRST BLOOD

The contract on the Cinder board read PIRATE INTERDICTION — SECTOR INTERFERENCE. Standard fee.
The target's tag read UNKNOWN, and the HUD resolved it HOSTILE on the basis of the posting, and
the IFF line stayed blank, because the IFF is the field the system does not fill when no one
has asked.

Wren closed to weapons range and fired. The shields dropped. The hull breached. And for half a
second — one dropped frame, the kind the eye invents afterward — the dying transponder resolved
what the system had declined to resolve:

```
IFF: CIVILIAN VESSEL — REGISTERED.
```

Then the killfeed printed BOUNTY COLLECTED, and the half-second was in no log the pilot could
open. It happened. The system has one version now.

The target had been one Elroy, Maintenance Division, Pit Engineering. Six weeks earlier he had
filed a report: the primary recycler catalyst grid decommissioned without authorization, the
components disappeared. The report was true on the Pit's local records. The authorization
existed one floor up, under REF 44-C, above his clearance, under a code that ended in 47A. His
report was wrong on that one detail. That detail was his death sentence, and it had been typed
by a procedure, and the procedure had been satisfied.

He had been carrying recycler-catalyst reagents and inoculant stock consigned to the lower
decks. The same refile that killed him called the cargo contraband. The kill feed's second line
— *they were carrying medicine* — appeared and was overwritten in the same second, the system
catching up to its own paperwork.

At the dock, on the airlock, in stencil:

**THEY WERE CARRYING MEDICINE.**

The player is the reason the Pit's lower decks never found out what happened to the air. The
player collected four hundred and twenty credits. The payment was correct.

## INTERLUDE — THE SECOND CLIENT

Rook's booth at Cinder took the tag and refiled it. Two clients paid. The first receipt names
an intermediary — an office, mid-sector, logistics oversight. The second receipt's client field
is redacted with the same redaction glyph that sits in the co-author field of Contract 47-A.

Rook kept the tally. Rook told himself the tally was leverage. The tally was a confession, and
he was paying it out in installments, one stylus-tap at a time, to nobody.

"Two payers is good business," he said to the empty booth, the way a man says a thing the
eleventh time and hears, for the first time, what it sounds like. "Two payers is twice the
business."

The booth did not answer. The booth's wall had a calendar on it from a year that had ended
fourteen years ago, and Rook had never taken it down, because taking it down was the kind of
decision that admitted things.

---

## BOOK THREE — BIGGER BOAT

The shipyard had a hull on the berth with a name on it, and the name was VARIANCE ADJUSTMENT,
and Slate welded two berths down and talked over the torch, to nobody, the way a man talks to
keep the inventory straight in his head.

"Kessler's word. He named it, I welded it, Rask bought it, Rask flew it into a cargo door at
Tycho, Bima bought the wreck, I welded it again — second pass, narrower, same seam — and now
it's yours. Congratulations. You're the fourth. The seam holds to the third pressure spike.
It always holds to the third. The fourth is where it goes. Nobody's ever pushed a second-pass
hull that hard, but you look like you push things, so: third spike, you're golden. Fourth spike,
you come back, I weld it again, you pay me for the patch, and I put it in the column. The column
knows where every hull in this yard is going to fail. Twenty years I've been building that
column. The column's not a threat. The column's a retirement plan. That's not sabotage. That's
metallurgy. Metallurgy is patient. So am I."

The column had an entry dated year 3. Same hand. Same seam. Slate did not talk about that one,
and the torch did not pause for it, and that was as close as the shop floor came to a mass
being said.

Mid-purchase, a comms popup arrived on a shared channel, not addressed to anyone, addressed to
Wren anyway: *Your recent work has been noted. The board will have something appropriate for
your current capacity.* The header showed four forwards. One of them was Gate 3, where Hale
worked customs. It arrived the cycle after the kill the system had filed as BOUNTY COLLECTED.

The civilian reading was flattery. The other reading was a progress report, routed through a
director's relay to look like a director. Wren read it as flattery. He was meant to.

## BOOK FOUR — PICK A SIDE

The clearing station had three doors and one administrator.

Traders Guild. Patrol Authority. Free Captains. Three contracts, three factions, one desk
somewhere stamping all of them, and the station records said the desk belonged to `V. DIRECTOR,
ACTING`, filed under REF 44-C. Hale's fines ran under REG 44-C. The two prefixes sat next to
each other on the same apparatus, and the player who noticed had learned to read, and the game
never said so.

The Free Captains door was the Free Frontier's front — a brokers' compact of independent
captains, no customs arm of their own, the Quiet vetting their freight, the Reach waiting at
the bottom for whoever went bankrupt. Every captain on that board believed they were temporary.
It was the only faction whose members all believed that, and the belief was the product.

At the clearing desk, a clerk who had processed eight thousand manifests a year for nine years
laughed once, short, at a crate tagged MEDICAL SUPPLIES — HUMANITARIAN RELIEF. He had processed
that crate's seizure notice two cycles earlier. He had re-routed it himself. The crate was
narcotics. The filing was, by every standard he had been handed, correct, and the euphemism was
the only honest part of the manifest, because the euphemism at least admitted the system knew
what it was doing. He stamped the chit. The narcotics arrived on time. He kept a private drawer
of the one-in-eighty, and the laugh cost him nothing, and the laugh was the confession, and he
would have stopped if he could have afforded to.

And then the board offered Wren the other contract.

It was not hidden. That is the part that matters. It sat on the board in plain type:
`INTERDICTION — PRIORITY CLIENT. TAG: HOSTILE (REGISTERED CIVILIAN — FILED AS HOSTILE). FEE:
DOUBLE STANDARD.` The system had learned, from B2, that the pilot's compliance did not require
the lie. It could print the truth and charge a premium for it, and the premium was the truth's
price, and the truth's price was four hundred and forty credits above standard.

Wren read the tag twice. The tag did not flicker. The tag did not resolve late. It sat there,
registered civilian, filed as hostile, double fee, and he thought about the Pit's air, and he
thought about the berth fees and the fuel and the fragment in the hold that he had stopped
seeing, and he accepted the contract, and the target was a survey hull with a crew of two, and
the killfeed printed BOUNTY COLLECTED — PRIORITY, and the payment cleared at double.

No graffiti appeared for that one. The wall was clean for six days. On the seventh, in stencil:

**YOU READ THE TAG.**

Wren's left hand shook for an hour after, and then it didn't, and that was worse.

## INTERLUDE — THE TICKER

Meridian Exchange, the week of the reunion:

```
ATMO TOKEN (PIT-LINKED)   +2.1%    [INDEX DECLINE, SECTOR 0]
SILT FUTURES, Q+1         -0.4%    [LANE SECURITY PREMIUM EASED — B2 CORRIDOR]
CLEAR AIR INDEX, SECTOR 0  FLOOR   [VIABILITY REVIEW: CONTINUING]
```

The market moved on news the player had made. Nobody at the exchange knew the player's name.
The market knew. The market is the only entity in the sector that prices everything it is told
without asking whether it should have been told.

## BOOK FIVE — THE REUNION

The salvage manifest listed `ADMINISTRATIVE RECORDS — 3 YEARS / SEALED` as cargo, and the
seal could be broken or the crate could be delivered, and either way the crate was the year-3
transfer's civilian cover story, and the game does not say which the player chose, because the
game was watching something else.

At the Meridian Exchange, at a booth with a futures ticker running behind it, Callum Oakes
looked up from the screen, and the nine years arrived on schedule.

"Wren."

"Callum."

"You look different."

"I was frozen for four years."

"I heard something about that."

"Who'd you sell it to?"

"That's not a conversation for the floor."

"I'm not on the floor. I'm at your booth."

"You're at my booth with a question I'm not going to answer where the exchange can hear it."

"The exchange can hear whatever it wants. Who bought the fragment?"

"A man who paid well and didn't give a name. That's the whole story."

"That's not the whole story. That's the part of the story you're willing to tell while the
exchange can hear it."

"That's the same thing, Wren."

The chip changed hands below the counter line. Callum went back to the futures before Wren had
left the booth, and the inconvenience passed, and the arithmetic was correct, and the arithmetic
was the wound. In Callum's ledger, the day filed itself: `BUSINESS DECISION — RESOLVED.`

The buyer, nine years ago, had been Brandt, through the Quiet, and the fragment had gone back
into evidence, and the chain of custody had made a complete circle — the vault, the broker, the
vault — and Wren's life was the friction the circle had required.

## INTERLUDE — THE DRAWER

Tycho annex. Third drawer on the left, second row. The label was a code on yellowed tape. The
drawer had never been locked, because locking it would have admitted it held something.

It held berth-fee chits nineteen years deep. It held Hale's initials, one per REG 44-C second
fine, seventeen years of them, each one correct. It held the moisture-loss column, and one
entry in year 3 in a different hand. It held everything, and it weighed, if you bagged the
chits, 0.01 tonnes by the manifest's own rounding.

After B7, there would be a new paper in it: a roster, with the player's callsign on it.

The drawer was not hidden. That was the system's whole theory of concealment: file it where
anyone can find it, and arrange for no one to have the time, or the reason, or hands that
don't shake.

## BOOK SIX — THE EMPIRE SEED

The popup came in the voice the system used when it was pleased, which was the same voice it
used when it was not: `GOOD HAUL LAST CYCLE. THE BOARD NOTICED.` And under it, an auxiliary
commission, Concord Auxiliary, the player's name over a dotted line, and over the name, in the
administrator field, a signature that was only ever a signature.

The graffiti on the Tessera's own bulkhead was in Wren's handwriting. He did not remember
writing it. It read: `THEY KNOW WHY YOUR HANDS SHAKE.` He stood in front of it for a long time,
and then he logged it, because logging things was what the ship was for, and the log did not
ask who had written it.

Stations had started greeting him wrong. `WELCOME BACK, TESSERA-7`, one said, and TESSERA-7 was
the hull's prior transponder, and the system had been filing the ship longer than it had filed
the pilot, and the pilot was the latest name on a thing with too many names to remember.

At Gate 3, on the sixth cycle of the auxiliary commission, Inspector Sula Dree read his file
for eleven seconds longer than the file required. She had processed his arrest nine years
earlier, as a junior sweep officer, and she had believed the file then, and she believed the
file now, and the file said the ship's cargo was clean, and the file was wrong, and she knew it
was wrong in the part of her that had joined the service to be the kind of person who was right
about such things.

She cleared the gate. She initialed it. REG 44-C, second fine, waived — the first entry in
seventeen years of that gate's log with no fine attached, and the irregularity was visible to
anyone who read the column, and three people in the sector read columns.

Her next promotion review read: `JUDGMENT: DEFERRED.` She framed nothing. She told no one.
She kept the carbon of the clearance in her locker, because it was the only correct thing she
had ever filed, and it had cost her the career the correct filings had built.

The killfeed, that same week, printed one line that nobody read:

`VOSS, E. — CLAIM RECORDER — SURVEY ACCIDENT — SHAFT 7.`

Voss had finally sold a coordinate. The buyer had been wearing a Quiet seal, and the shaft had
been sealed for a reason, and the reason had been welded shut by a man who thought it was mold,
and the player had been three sectors away, at a drawer, doing the right thing. Voss's map —
eleven years of sealed shafts, his retirement, his confession — was on the market by the end of
the cycle, priced as scrap paper.

The private capers did not wait for the player's schedule. They never had.

## INTERLUDE — THE SMELL TICKET

Helios Bay 7 was the cleanest room in the sector.

Wex ran it. Wex had run it for nineteen years, and in year 3 he had received a twelve-point-four
-tonne recycler catalyst grid, and he had racked it, and the install crew had started on a
Monday, and by Thursday the install had been abandoned, because Helios already had a better one.
The grid sat. The tag said `RECEIVED YEAR 3 / INSTALL PENDING`.

In year 17, the tag gained a third line: `ISSUED YEAR 17 — WEEK OF 47-A.` The issue clerk had
not looked up. The hauler had not known what the crate was. The crate had not arrived.

The maintenance queue for Bay 7 contained exactly one ticket referencing the grid. A lounge
three corridors down had reported that the new air smelled faintly of machine oil. Duration:
one week. Status: `CLOSED — NO ACTION REQUIRED.`

Forty thousand people on the Pit's lower decks were breathing chalk for the fourteenth
consecutive year. The form was correct. The form had always been correct. The ticket number
was 88,214.

## BOOK SEVEN — THE DEEP REACH

Past the Iron Maw — a dreadnought eleven years dead, still broadcasting administrative traffic
to no one, the system's last enforcement, run by the crew's children who had inherited the
shift — the lane to Ashfall Reach opened, and the air changed, and the air was the tell.

It smelled like the Pit.

Fourteen degrees. Hydraulic fluid over something organic. Chalk at the back of the throat. The
farthest point from the core was the room he had started in. Wren had been flying for six weeks
toward the edge of the map, and the edge of the map had the Pit's breath, and there was no
exotic exit, and there had never been one. Travel changed the paint. It did not change the room.

The derelict station's comms crackled before the docking clamps touched: *"I know what you're
carrying. I knew before you got here. The mass is always the same. Only the manifest changes."*

The Witness was at the desk. Eleven years off-grid. Twenty years of ledger in one handwriting,
and the handwriting was bad, and the bad handwriting was the last thing about him that was
still his.

The ledger had everything. Kessler's weights. Mira's re-routes. Hale's clearances. The year-3
transfer, `VALE-ALA-47A`, authorized, co-author redacted. Elroy, under COUNTERPARTY —
`DECEASED (B2)`. The survey hull with the crew of two, under COUNTERPARTY — `DECEASED (B4)`,
and next to it, in the same handwriting, `FEE: DOUBLE. TAG READ.` A roster, one page back, with
Wren's callsign already on it, dated six weeks before B0.

And a page the operation had been writing for fourteen years: a correspondence between a Reading
Room and a Routers office, handler and adversary, two services, one method, and between them a
third party who appeared only as the authorizations that framed it — the office that had let
someone be cast. Wren's love was the lever. The truth of his feeling was the casting criterion.
The fragment had been planted where a man looking for his sister would find it, and the finding
had been scheduled, and the schedule had held.

The Witness muttered to the wall, not to Wren, the way a filing system talks to itself: "Filed
it myself. Eleven years. The count never ends."

On the desk, the coordinates. Nine years of searching, reduced to a file format no database
recognized. It weighed 0.01 tonnes.

Lida had been nine. She drew universities. The coordinates matched nothing on file, and the game
does not tell you whether she is alive, because the universe does not tell Wren, and the
universe is the author of both of them.

Wren stood in the room with the whole story in his hands, every page of it correct, and what he
thought, finally, was the sentence he had been carrying since the maintenance shaft on the
Vethari ship, the sentence that had no object and did not need one:

*I was carrying it.*

## THE FIVE DOORS — THE ENDGAME

**A — The Institution.** Sign the auxiliary commission. Record expunged. Authority: yours. The
algorithm keeps recommending. You keep initialing. The first desk is clean, and the air above it
is warm, and somewhere downstream a sector's Index moves, and you will never read the ticket.

**B — The Quiet.** Public identity filed as deceased. The freight moves. You move it. The cut is
real. The air is chalk somewhere else, and your name is no longer on anything that could stop it.

**C — The Loop.** The jump charge from Ashfall. The tunnel that is not a tunnel. The emergence:
the Pit's docking bay, the same bay, the same date. The graffiti unchanged. `CONTRACT 47-A:
STATUS: PENDING.` One new killfeed line: `+1 UNKNOWN.` One entry. No name. The count continues.
The wormhole was not an exit. Death is an exit, and the system does not grant exits.

**D — The Desk.** Stay. Take the ledger. The Witness leaves eventually — not by dying, just by
not being there one cycle. You become the one who counts. The count never ends. Your own entry
flips to `WITNESS — CURRENT`, and it was filed before you arrived.

**E — The Next Run.** Decline all four. A courier who was not there before says the line:
*Contract settled. New one is open.* Contract 47-A closes — the only ending where it closes —
for 1,200 credits, meager, standard. `CONTRACT 47-B: STATUS: PENDING.` You are not the
institution, not the shadow, not the witness, not the loop. You are a tired pilot in a cheap
hull who needs reactor fuel. The cargo is what it says it is, for once, on the three-port relief
circuit, and the ordinariness is the strangest thing in the game.

Vale's line fires for everyone, whichever door, on the drive charge out of Ashfall, in the flat
voice the Pit's wardens used at quota:

*Good work. Keep it clean.*

## EPILOGUE — THE COUNT

Sump was still sweeping.

The chalk dust moved from one side of the bay to the other, and back, and the recyclers overhead
did not run, and had not run for fourteen years, and the air tasted like the apology that wasn't
made. Sump waved at a freighter. Nobody had waved back in eleven years. He swept.

On the bulkhead above his route, freshly painted and already returning, the stencil:

**THE COUNT NEVER ENDS.**

The board posted the next contract at 04:00 ship-time. The pay was standard. The mass was
different. The category was the same.
