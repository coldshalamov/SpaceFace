I’d actually go even stranger. The first interactive alien species should force the player to learn a new grammar of gameplay. Humans teach you reputation. Pirates teach you violence. Markets teach you price. An alien should make some familiar variable suddenly meaningless.

For example, imagine a species with no individual ships at all. What you think are ships are transient organs extruded by a distributed organism occupying several asteroid belts. Destroying one produces no reputation loss because it isn't regarded as an individual. But changing an asteroid's orbit, taking particular isotopes, or emitting a certain radar pattern causes the entire organism to react. You eventually realize you've been communicating with it for hours through your flight behavior. No dialogue wheel. No universal translator. You learn its syntax empirically.

That feels like SpaceFace.

And there’s an even better structural opportunity here: aliens could turn the galaxy from a collection of sectors into a place with layers of epistemology. Imagine an organism—call it a xenophage for now—that arrived in this region millions of years ago. Its defining evolutionary innovation is unusually aggressive horizontal gene transfer: instead of waiting for mutation + selection over generations, it incorporates useful biological machinery from organisms it parasitizes.

That gives you a beautiful rule:

It cannot invent quickly. It can steal quickly.

So every population tells you something about what it has encountered.

A xenophage living around a sulfur-rich moon might have metabolized extremophile chemistry. Another population that infected armored megafauna develops enormous keratinous plating. One that got into a photosynthetic organism unfolds solar membranes when idle. One that infected something using electroreception starts sensing ships through their power systems. One that encounters humans eventually becomes much more disturbing.

That means the creature design itself becomes environmental storytelling.

You scan one and find:

STRUCTURAL PROTEIN: UNKNOWN
METABOLIC PATHWAY: LOCAL FAUNA MATCH 81%
NEURAL PROTEIN: HUMAN HOMOLOG — 17%

And suddenly the scanner has told a horror story.

They're not evil.

They're astonishingly competent evolution.

A human sees infection, mutilation and assimilation. The xenophage is merely doing what mitochondria, viruses, plasmids and transposons have been doing on Earth forever—except with the accelerator slammed through the floor.

And then you can make the mechanism somewhat scientifically respectable.

Real biology already performs horizontal gene transfer. Bacteria swap plasmids. Viruses shuttle DNA between organisms. Endogenous retroviruses comprise a substantial fraction of animal genomes. Some organisms steal functional biological machinery from prey. Evolution is much less respectful of species boundaries than the clean branches in textbooks suggest.

SpaceFace's alien version could have evolved an absurdly sophisticated system of:

host sampling → sequence acquisition → regulatory integration → developmental experimentation → selection.

The crucial constraint should be that most acquisitions are garbage.

Otherwise it's biological magic.

Maybe only 0.01% of captured sequences become useful, and colonies run huge numbers of grotesque developmental experiments. That explains the mutant forms: they're not deliberately engineered soldiers. They're evolutionary scratch paper.

And there is a fantastic gameplay system hiding inside that.

Every local xenophage population maintains a trait pool.

If it encounters:

shielded ships, selection favors electromagnetic sensing;
laser weapons, reflective/cooling tissue gets selected;
kinetic weapons, thicker sacrificial armor emerges;
mining vessels, it might acquire tolerance to industrial radiation;
livestock or native megafauna, entirely new movement/body plans appear.
Not instant Borg adaptation. That gets silly.

Instead, actions alter ecological selection pressures over many encounters.

The player might leave a region and return twenty hours later and realize:

"Oh fuck. These things are different now."

That is much scarier.

It also produces a potentially magnificent SpaceFace mechanic: the player can accidentally teach the ecosystem how to kill them.

Kill enough of them with one tactic and you're performing artificial selection.

Now we're cooking.

The core rule
The fungus should not be:

a magic intelligence booster
a universal “make monster” goo
or a perfect hivemind with no tradeoffs
It should be something more specific and more disturbing:

it links nervous systems, but at a cost.

in less intelligent animals, it creates crude instinct-sharing, migratory urges, pack behavior, aggression loops, and body-plan mutations
in damaged hosts, it creates partial or broken connection: fragments of memory, phantom impulses, foggy directional behavior, ritual repetition, inherited fear, inherited attraction

I looked through the live repo architecture, and there’s a workable route. SpaceFace already has most of the scaffolding we need: named sector zones in src/data/sectorZones.js, deterministic world materialization in world.js, mystery encounters in encounterDirector.js, scanner/anomaly behavior, the tactical AI stack, pooled VFX, narrative triggers, persistent world state, and the authored GLB pipeline. We should make those systems express the alien ecology rather than build a second game beside them.

There is also one thing we should clean up before anything else: the repo already contains a live faction_understory, described as a xenomorphic saprophyte with “spore bloom on death,” wreck scavenging and fungus-like ships, plus the planned Mycelia world. That now collides directly with the stronger Vethari-fungus cosmology we just arrived at. I would merge/retcon that material rather than allow SpaceFace to develop two unrelated fungal civilizations.

Do a brutally small vertical slice. Before building an ecology, prove the entire pipeline with one infected location. I’d use an existing derelict_field or anomaly_deep zone because those are already recognized by the encounter director.

Build content multipliers, not content. This is where most of the eventual savings are.
For fungus, I’d make perhaps 8–12 reusable authored modules: filament sheets, branching veins, nodal bulbs, spore sacs, calcified masses, tendril clusters, membrane patches, hull-entry lesions, etc. The runtime can deterministically compose them over existing wrecks, stations and rocks.

That means we don’t need twenty “fungal station” GLBs. We need one infestation-dressing system that can make twenty existing locations look biologically colonized.

Same idea for fauna: build a handful of morphological systems rather than thirty bespoke monsters.

And here is a major engineering constraint worth respecting: SpaceFace is presently overwhelmingly a rigid-ship game. A conventional animal bestiary suddenly introduces skeletal animation, skinning, animation blending, foot placement and a lot of rendering/tooling baggage.

So the first space fauna should deliberately have locomotion that works with the engine:

drifting jelly/sac organisms;
segmented worm forms;
manta-like vacuum swimmers;
radial organisms;
tentacled grapplers;
colonial swarms.
Those can look extremely alien while using procedural deformation, articulated pieces, shader motion and ordinary rigid-body movement. We postpone “four-legged mutated alien tiger running around inside stations” until the game actually needs an animation stack.

Create an ecology system, but keep its simulation small. The fauna do not need human tactical AI. Give them drives:
drift → forage → investigate → avoid → defend → swarm → attach → flee

Then add hive-field modifiers.

A nearby relay organism might increase coordination. Kill it and the animals don’t suddenly turn off; their behavior becomes noisier, less synchronized and more primitive.

Likewise the “shared memory” should initially be represented by behavioral priors, not some giant learning simulation. A local strain can possess tags such as:

reactor_attraction
beam_avoidance
station_nesting
ancient_signal_aversion
predator_memory

Later, if we love the mechanic, those traits can actually change from player actions. We do not need to build Darwinian Dwarf Fortress on day one.

Then build the contamination gradient across existing space. This is where the idea becomes an arc rather than a creature pack.
Early sectors: almost nothing.

Outer sectors: suspicious growth on wrecks, unusual organisms, quarantine markers.

Veil-ish territory: functioning alien ecology.

Deeper space: abandoned infrastructure increasingly colonized.

Near-domain: entire locations become composite biological/technological ruins.

Very deep: the normal visual vocabulary of SpaceFace begins to lose the argument.

Critically, we can do much of this by variant-dressing things the game already owns. Existing stations, wrecks, asteroids and zones are raw material.

There is one other hard constraint: performance. SpaceFace’s own performance campaign says the current machine is especially vulnerable to transient CPU hitches, and the renderer already has deliberate pooling/zero-allocation conventions. So alien ecology cannot mean “spawn 200 independently ticking tentacles.” Dense infection needs instancing, pooled VFX, cheap distant simulation and entity LOD from the beginning. Otherwise the cosmic horror will be the frame-time graph.

A few ideas became much stronger while putting the whole thing together.

The Understory retcon is probably the cleanest. We don't have to throw away the work already done. faction_understory can remain a technical namespace for encounters/IFF/content routing, but fictionally “The Understory” becomes Concord/spacer shorthand for these colonized wreck ecologies. Its existing rule that it only re-flies hulls from the loss ledger becomes perfect: the fungus doesn't manufacture starships; it colonizes things that actually died.

The precursor idea also clicked beautifully with the existing Verge-Layers. Rather than inventing another ancient alien faction, the Verge-Layers become the surviving machine institutions of the legendary race. One might spend eighteen thousand years watching one contaminated moon because its directive says witness. Another might consider a human station an unauthorized modification to a gate and quietly begin dismantling it. They aren't gods because they talk like gods. They're gods because they can move an asteroid and then issue a three-line maintenance notice.

And I put a pretty severe production discipline into the plan: do not authorize the huge alien content wave until the Charon slice is genuinely excellent. If one derelict can't make the player think “what the fuck is happening here?” through behavior, sound, geometry and interaction rather than exposition, fifty species won't save us.

That seems like the right place to put the knife before we start pouring money and agents into this thing.
