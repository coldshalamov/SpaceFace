## First: fuel, munitions, and fleets

Fuel is not the same as boost energy. It is currently a jump-navigation resource: the world system uses a base cost of four fuel units per light-year, modified by jump-drive tier. Whether that resource deserves to exist is still debatable, but that is what it is for.

Munitions are also narrower than the station UI makes them sound. In the weapon catalog, normal lasers, autocannons, railguns, plasma weapons, and the siege lance use energy and/or heat. Only missile racks, the unique Nestbreaker rack, and torpedoes specify `cmdty_munitions`.

That is potentially a good arrangement:

* Normal weapons remain permanently available.
* Advanced explosive weapons consume manufactured ordnance.
* Munitions become an optional conversion of industrial capacity into combat power.
* Stations should automatically replenish them, or let the player fabricate them in bulk—not force them to buy bullets after every fight.

**Need should be strategic, not hygienic.**

There is also already an agent-created wingman system in the repo. It spawns fleet-ledger ships into the world, maps orders onto existing AI archetypes, and synchronizes their damage back to the ledger. But even its mining order maps to a defensive trader archetype because there is no actual mining AI.

So your instinct is sound: **do not make fleets the next major project.** The underlying world is not yet interesting enough to command a fleet around, and the AI would amplify existing problems.

## The actual structural problem

The repo already contains “named zones” specifically intended to prevent sectors from feeling like flat test rooms. The file literally describes the old problem as “a flat disc of unrelated dots” and defines civilian cores, trade lanes, mining belts, derelict fields, outlaw zones, anomaly zones, and so forth.

But those zones are primarily metadata: circles, labels, faction associations, spawn weights, and reasons for existing.

The regional ecology system is even more explicit. It says it does not spawn anything, damage anything, authorize hostility, write resources, or produce ambient activity. It mostly adjusts traffic, resource, law, danger, and encounter weights.

The POI behavior layer has six supposedly different families, but their underlying verbs are:

* Dock
* Mine
* Salvage
* Scan three times
* Escort
* Kill two enemies

So the game has accumulated many names for things without accumulating many **kinds of physical interaction**.

The wreck system is the most brutal example. The authored unique-wreck data is actually sophisticated: rumor channels, fuzzy bearings, scanning requirements, hazards, unique weapons, provenance, follow-ups, and recovery consequences.

But the ordinary aftermath-wreck runtime collapses the result into one entity:

* Type: `wreck`
* Radius: **9**
* Salvage duration: **8 seconds**
* One generic salvage pool
* One position

That is the whole disease in miniature. A recorded battle, a dead named vessel, legal provenance, a black box, regional consequences, and a salvage mission all collapse into **one radius-nine blob that you hold a button against**.

The claimable-body system has the same mismatch. Its underlying design—depots, refineries, teleporters, defense batteries, trade relays—is almost exactly aligned with your desired mining-to-automation progression. But many claim sites reuse ordinary asteroid meshes or a conveyor barge, with visual radii of only 20–32 units.

The design is trying to say **industrial moon**. The screen is saying **slightly important rock**.

## What the game needs: a World Site layer

Do not ask agents for more wrecks, planets, stations, or anomalies yet. They will produce more balls, boxes, labels, and reward tables.

First build one generalized system for **large, stateful, multi-component sites**.

A site should have:

* A large physical footprint
* Several visually distinct components
* Multiple possible interactions
* A persistent state
* A relationship to the economy, story, navigation, or combat
* A visible before-and-after transformation

Every major site should answer four questions:

1. **What can I physically do here?**
2. **What changes after I do it?**
3. **What larger system does it feed?**
4. **What does it reveal about the world?**

And every major site should support at least **two different verbs**, not merely “hold RMB until materials appear.”

A compact interaction vocabulary would be enough:

* Scan
* Cut
* Tether
* Tow
* Activate
* Repair
* Deploy
* Collect
* Claim
* Link

Those ten verbs, recombined intelligently, could produce far more depth than fifty bespoke systems.

## The kinds of things SpaceFace should contain

### 1. Monumental wreck complexes

Not a “wreck entity.” A **wreck site**.

A destroyed cruiser might be 300–500 world units long, broken into an engine block, command hull, cargo spine, detached armor slabs, weapon mounts, and scattered compartments. Most pieces can remain static; only a few need to be tetherable.

The player could:

* Fly through the split hull
* Scan compartments to locate powered systems
* Cut braces with the mining beam
* Pull armor or cargo modules free with the massline
* Tow a reactor or weapon assembly
* Recover a black box
* Restore emergency power
* Find a unique manufacturing blueprint
* Later establish an automated salvage operation

The wreck should persist as a stripped skeleton after the player finishes. The world remembers.

This immediately gives the massline, scanner, mining beam, cargo system, crafting, story system, and automation system something new to do—without creating six unrelated minigames.

### 2. Industrial sites

Build refinery complexes, extraction platforms, shipbreaking yards, cargo depots, orbital foundries, abandoned mines, and construction scaffolds.

These are ideal because they feed your actual game spine:

> Mine manually → deploy drones → establish storage → process materials → fabricate machines → expand production.

An abandoned refinery could begin cold and damaged. The player repairs three substations, delivers starter material, reconnects the output arm, and claims it. It then becomes a functioning automation node.

Its transformation should be visible:

* Dark structure becomes illuminated
* Conveyor arms start moving
* Drones appear
* Cargo containers accumulate
* Haulers begin visiting
* Additional modules physically attach to it

That visual growth is more valuable than another passive-income number in a menu.

### 3. Planetary and orbital operations

You do not need planet landing, walking, cinematics, or fully rendered surfaces.

Make planets interactive through things **around them**:

* Orbital elevator terminals
* Satellite fields
* Atmospheric harvesters
* Surface-to-orbit cargo launchers
* Mining rings
* Moon installations
* Research platforms
* Colony traffic
* Defense arrays
* Abandoned orbital habitats

The planet becomes a gravitational or visual anchor for a set of space-game interactions.

Possible actions include:

* Scan the surface from several orbital positions
* Deploy a survey drone
* Retrieve a launched cargo capsule
* Install an atmospheric extractor
* Repair a communications constellation
* Build an orbital refinery
* Defend a colony evacuation
* Link the planet into the player’s logistics network

This is how you get “planet interaction” without accidentally beginning development of *No Man’s Sky 2: Token Bankruptcy Edition*.

### 4. Infrastructure that changes traversal

The world needs structures whose value is not materials or combat.

Examples:

* Navigation relays
* Broken acceleration rings
* Jump stabilizers
* Customs checkpoints
* Mass-driver cargo launchers
* Sensor arrays
* Player-built beacons
* Refueling pylons
* Wormhole anchors
* Defense platforms

Repairing or claiming infrastructure could:

* Open a shortcut
* Reveal distant sectors
* Reduce travel time
* Increase local traffic
* Extend drone range
* Improve market knowledge
* Protect convoys
* Allow automation across sectors

This makes expansion feel like taking control of the galaxy rather than merely raising an income statistic.

### 5. Environmental machinery and anomalies

Hazards should not only be damage circles.

Examples:

* A moving ion storm concealing valuable wrecks
* A gravitational eddy that bends projectiles and flight paths
* A crystal formation that resonates when pieces are moved
* A dormant alien ring that must be aligned with the massline
* A debris current carrying objects through the sector
* A periodically opening radiation corridor
* A colossal machine emitting navigational pulses
* An anomaly that duplicates or displaces nearby salvage

These can be mostly deterministic state machines. They do not require advanced AI.

### 6. Visible civilian and industrial activity

NPCs do not need brilliant intelligence to stop feeling identical. They need **different jobs with visible routes**.

A miner should:

1. Travel to a field.
2. Remain near asteroids.
3. Produce or collect ore.
4. Travel to a refinery or hauler.

A hauler should travel from production site to station. A scavenger should visit wrecks. A patrol should follow a lane and checkpoint. A survey vessel should circle anomalies and emit scans.

These can be waypoint loops and simple state machines. Combat AI only takes over when something attacks.

That would produce much more life than giving every NPC a more sophisticated dogfighting brain while they continue wandering around without a reason.

## How sectors should be composed

A sector should not be “one pile containing every available object type.”

It should contain several geographically separated pockets connected by traffic or navigation:

* A destination or civic core
* A production region
* A logistics route
* A danger or contested region
* A mystery, ruin, or landmark

But do not put all five in every sector. That would merely create a larger template.

Instead, build sector archetypes:

* Industrial belt
* Trade junction
* Ship graveyard
* Border checkpoint
* Anomaly field
* Pirate-held ruin
* Construction frontier
* Quiet sanctuary
* Active war zone
* Player expansion region

Each sector needs one sentence that no other sector can claim:

* “This is the sector with the broken moon refinery.”
* “This is the sector built around a kilometer-long carrier wreck.”
* “This is the customs gauntlet where every convoy is scanned.”
* “This is the storm sector where wrecks surface only during clear intervals.”
* “This is the place where my automated empire began.”

One major silhouette should be recognizable before the player reaches it. A sector landmark should not be radius 20. It may need to be hundreds of world units across, with distant lighting and navigation relevance.

## Story without cutscenes or dialogue trees

Your preferences point toward a much better narrative model for this game:

**No response menus. No mutually exclusive branches. No lengthy conversations. No requirement to stop playing.**

Use three layers.

### Ambient fragments

One or two lines appear briefly during flight:

> HULL REGISTRY: VIGILANT
> OFFICIAL STATUS: DESTROYED 43 YEARS AGO

Or:

> EMERGENCY POWER DETECTED
> THREE COMPARTMENTS STILL PRESSURIZED

These should fade automatically. The player never has to click “Continue.”

### Inspection fragments

Scanning, cutting open, repairing, or recovering a component reveals another short fragment. The story is paced by physical investigation.

### Persistent ledger

Everything discovered is quietly stored in a ship ledger, codex, or map annotation. Players who care can read it later. Players who do not care never become trapped in a dialogue box.

Story threads should remain open. Completing one thread may change the order, context, reward, or current state of another, but it should not permanently delete half the game.

The choice is not:

> “Do you support Faction A or Faction B?”

The choice is embodied:

* Restore the station
* Strip it for parts
* Leave it dormant
* Build beside it
* Return later

And even those actions need not permanently remove all remaining content.

## Make automation the narrative arc

The progression you described is stronger than a conventional campaign:

1. **Discover** a resource or abandoned site.
2. **Extract** from it manually.
3. **Repair or claim** infrastructure.
4. **Deploy** drones and storage.
5. **Process** raw material.
6. **Fabricate** more capable machines.
7. **Connect** multiple sites.
8. **Defend** the network.
9. **Transform** entire sectors.
10. **Control** the regional economy and movement of ships.

That is both gameplay and story.

The game begins with you as one person scraping ore from rocks. It ends with the night side of a moon glowing with machinery you built, convoys moving along routes you opened, and defense platforms protecting an economy that did not exist before you arrived.

No payroll spreadsheet required.

I would also remove or soften the current per-minute upkeep instincts in the claim system. A refinery that lacks input can simply stop producing. A trade relay without cargo can remain idle. Infrastructure should not constantly decay because the player looked away. Exceptional attacks and failures can happen, but they should be events—not galactic whack-a-mole.

## One physics caveat that matters

A stationary gravity well cannot give you permanent speed.

It accelerates you as you fall inward, but you lose that same speed climbing back out. At the same distant radius, your speed is unchanged; only the direction of your velocity has changed. A real gravity assist provides net energy because the planet itself is moving and you exchange momentum with it.

So a planetary slingshot system needs one of two deliberate designs:

1. **Moving-body gravity assist.** Give each planet an authored orbital-frame velocity. Passing behind its motion adds speed; passing ahead removes speed. This is the more physically meaningful option.

2. **Stylized exit transfer.** Gravity curves the flight honestly, and a bounded impulse is granted at a successful exit, representing momentum taken from the planet’s orbital motion. This is simpler and is consistent with the existing massline slingshot philosophy.

A stationary well is a steering wheel, not a battery. The game should not accidentally promise acceleration from a system that can only turn the ship.

# The foundational system: a gravity-field kernel

Build one generic `gravityFields` system before building any planet, dark-matter weapon, black hole, or orbital puzzle.

Each gravity source would have roughly:

```text
position
influenceRadius
surfaceRadius
atmosphereRadius
softeningRadius
mu / fieldStrength
maximumAcceleration
frameVelocity
fieldType
affectedEntityMask
```

For natural gravity, use a softened inverse-square field:

[
\mathbf a(\mathbf r)
====================

-\frac{\mu,\mathbf r}
{\left(r^2+\epsilon^2\right)^{3/2}}
]

Here, (\epsilon) prevents pathological acceleration near the center, and acceleration should also be capped. The player should never cross a numerical singularity and be fired into the next Git branch.

For artificial gravity weapons, a compact finite-radius falloff may be more controllable:

[
a(r)=A\left(1-\frac{r^2}{R^2}\right)^2,\qquad r<R
]

That gives you a strong, smooth field without the infinite tail of real gravity.

The governing restrictions should be:

* Massive bodies create fields, but ordinary bodies do not mutually attract each other.
* Planets do not react to ships.
* Only the strongest one or two nearby fields affect a body.
* Field sources are authored or deliberately deployed, not randomly attached to everything.
* The field is integrated at the fixed simulation timestep.
* The trajectory preview uses the exact same acceleration function as gameplay.
* Physics-earned velocity is treated separately from ordinary engine speed limiting.
* Field entry and exit use hysteresis so controls and UI do not flicker at the boundary.
* The player receives safety assists; NPCs receive honest physical consequences.

That gives the game gravity without turning it into an unstable (N)-body simulation.

# The primary gravity minigame: Gravity Ride

This should exist directly in the flight world. No modal screen, no dialogue, no press-E completion.

A planet has four readable regions:

### The influence zone

The outer region where gravity begins visibly curving trajectories. The starfield, dust, flight predictor, and orbiting traffic should make the field legible before it seriously affects the player.

### The useful-assist band

A wide annulus where the player receives meaningful trajectory bending without severe risk.

### The high-gain corridor

A narrower inner region where a deeper pass produces more deflection and a larger moving-body momentum transfer.

### The atmosphere or exclusion zone

Too deep produces drag, heat, shield stress, sensor disruption, or emergency extraction. This is not an invisible damage circle; its visual boundary should be unmistakable.

## What the player actually does

Before entering the well, the player targets a waypoint, station, planet, enemy, or general exit direction.

As the ship enters the influence zone, the game draws:

* A predicted trajectory as a world-space ribbon.
* A periapsis marker showing the closest predicted approach.
* An exit marker showing where the ship will leave the influence zone.
* A target gate representing the desirable exit corridor.
* Expected trajectory rotation.
* Estimated momentum gain or loss.
* Heat or atmospheric risk.
* A prograde/retrograde indication for the planet’s orbital motion.

The player’s task is to adjust the approach so that:

1. The predicted periapsis lies in the useful band.
2. The exit marker crosses the desired gate.
3. The ship does not overheat or fall into the exclusion zone.
4. The player uses as little corrective thrust as possible.

That is a real minigame. It is not merely “fly near planet, receive boost.”

## Controls that fit the top-down game

The cleanest assist uses a **planet-relative radial/tangential frame**.

Let:

[
\hat r=\frac{\mathbf p_{\text{ship}}-\mathbf p_{\text{planet}}}
{\left|\mathbf p_{\text{ship}}-\mathbf p_{\text{planet}}\right|}
]

and:

[
\hat t=\operatorname{perp}(\hat r)
]

Then decompose velocity into:

[
v_r=\mathbf v\cdot\hat r,\qquad
v_t=\mathbf v\cdot\hat t
]

An optional held Gravity Ride Assist could interpret controls as:

* **W:** prograde burn; increase orbital or exit speed.
* **S:** retrograde burn; reduce speed or help capture into orbit.
* **A/D:** bias the trajectory inward or outward.
* **No input:** coast under gravity.
* **Release assist:** immediately restores ordinary piloting.

The player chooses whether to dive, raise periapsis, accelerate, brake, or exit. The computer merely keeps the ship’s attitude and thrust vector coherent in the planet-relative frame.

That follows the strongest principle already in the massline design: the player supplies intent; the computer supplies precision.

This same radial/tangential controller can also solve your existing massline orbit problem. While tethered:

* The anchor supplies (\hat r).
* The chosen clockwise/counterclockwise direction supplies (\hat t).
* The controller maintains tangential thrust and corrects only enough radial velocity to keep the tether taut.
* Your ship no longer turns too quickly or too slowly relative to line length.
* Releasing the controls immediately returns full manual authority.

That is a much cleaner shared primitive than separate ad hoc “spin detectors” for every tether maneuver.

## Reward structure

A successful gravity ride can provide several rewards without becoming a generic score screen:

* A large directional change without fuel.
* Physics-earned speed that temporarily exceeds normal powered-flight speed.
* A `Momentum Chain` that persists into another well.
* Faster access to remote sector pockets.
* Better mission completion times.
* Stealthy engine-off travel.
* Kinetic weapon bonuses that scale with ship velocity.
* Access to orbital objects unreachable by brute-force thrust.
* Traffic shortcuts usable by player automation after discovery.

The important point is that gravity mastery should alter **where and how you can travel**, not only provide a ten-percent speed buff.

# Gravity and mass weapons

The best weapons should manipulate a physical variable rather than merely changing health.

## Inertial Shunt

A short-duration beam or projectile changes how strongly a target responds to impulses and massline forces.

A “lightened” enemy:

* Is easier to fling.
* Tumbles more easily.
* Is pushed farther by concussion weapons.
* Is more vulnerable to repulsor fields.
* Can be thrown into structures or atmosphere.

A “ballasted” player:

* Becomes a better temporary anchor.
* Resists knockback.
* Can drag a larger target.
* Loses agility and acceleration while active.

This is a powerful upgrade path because it changes relationships between existing mechanics. It is not “+25% damage.”

Internally, I would avoid changing every physical mass value indiscriminately. Give entities separate, explicit response terms such as:

```text
inertialResponse
tetherResponse
impulseResponse
gravityCoupling
```

That is less scientifically pure, but vastly more controllable than changing Rapier mass and discovering that the shipyard has launched into the sun.

## Concussion Cannon

A low-damage directional impulse weapon.

The impact direction matters. It can:

* Knock enemies into asteroids.
* Deflect attackers from a convoy.
* Push them into planetary atmosphere.
* Disrupt an enemy’s firing solution.
* Move debris into useful positions.
* Add velocity to a massline payload before release.

A heavier target moves less. An inertially-shunted target moves more. This is the simplest gravity-adjacent weapon and likely the highest-ROI combat addition.

## Gravity Mark

Instead of changing physical mass, this status increases a target’s coupling to artificial and natural gravity fields.

A marked enemy becomes much easier to pull into a well or atmosphere. The mark itself does not damage. It exists to enable combinations.

This avoids making every artificial gravity well overwhelmingly strong against every ship.

## Momentum Sink

A field or beam reduces velocity relative to a selected frame.

Possible frames:

* Relative to the player.
* Relative to a planet.
* Relative to a deployed mass seed.
* Relative to a convoy or station.

That creates:

* Capture tools.
* Boarding or salvage tools.
* Anti-missile defense.
* A way to stop a thrown object.
* A way to trap a fast enemy near a gravity well.

This should visibly transfer or dissipate momentum through a field effect rather than quietly setting velocity to zero.

## Tidal Fork

Two gravitic nodes create a steep field gradient between them.

Ships crossing the center receive opposing forces at different parts of the hull, causing:

* Rotation.
* Tumble.
* Weapon misalignment.
* Subsystem strain.
* Separation of loose cargo or detachable components.

This is advanced and should come after ordinary wells work. It is a good boss-scale or endgame tool, but a risky first feature.

## How the targeting concept is specified

The plan formalizes the three signals you identified:
