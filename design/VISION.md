# SPACEFACE — The Fun, the Fantasy, and the UVP

**Status: PRODUCT AUTHORITY — the owner's vision, in the owner's own words (2026-08-10).**
This document outranks every other document's *emphasis*. If anything in this repo — a doc, a
workflow, a habit — reads "Freelancer influence" as muted palettes or stately HP-bar combat, this
document overrides it. Read it before producing anything creative.

---

SpaceFace should feel like the player has been dropped into a busy little physical universe and
given permission to fuck with it.

The fantasy is not simply "fly a spaceship and shoot enemies." It is:

> **I understand how this world moves, and I can use that understanding to do ridiculous things.**

You are personally flying one ship. It has momentum. Other ships have mass. Asteroids are real
obstacles and anchors. Cargo is actually moving somewhere. Miners are actually working. Patrols are
actually patrolling. Pirates are actually hunting traffic.

And almost everything you see is something you can potentially interfere with.

That is the heart of the game.

## What makes SpaceFace fun

The player should constantly have moments where a simple action produces a much bigger, cooler
consequence than expected.

A pirate is chasing a freighter.

- You could just shoot him.
- Or you could hit him with a concussion weapon and send him tumbling into an asteroid.
- Or tether him with the Massline, use his momentum to swing around the asteroid, then release him
  into his own wingman.
- Or drop a gravity field into the formation, pull several light ships together, and fire something
  explosive into the mess.
- Or miss.

Now one of them has slammed into a civilian cargo pod. The patrol saw it. The cargo is floating
loose. The freighter is escaping. You are suddenly wanted. And the situation has become a
completely different story than the one you entered thirty seconds ago.

That is SpaceFace.

The goal is not tactical perfection. The goal is to repeatedly create:

> **"Holy shit, I did that."**

and occasionally:

> **"Oh fuck, I did that."**

Both are good. The world should react honestly to either one.

## The unique selling proposition

There are space games with better economies. There are games with deeper ship fitting. There are
games with more realistic physics. There are games with huge procedural galaxies. There are games
with elaborate campaigns.

SpaceFace does not need to beat them at those individual things. Its UVP is **the interaction
between physical agency and a living local world**.

SpaceFace is:

> **A fast, colorful, physics-driven space sandbox where you personally manipulate ships, momentum,
> tethers, gravity, cargo and terrain inside a working universe that remembers what you did.**

The physics matters because there are meaningful things around you to manipulate. The living world
matters because you can physically interfere with it. Neither half is enough by itself. A physics
sandbox with nothing around you becomes a tech demo. A living economy you only watch becomes an
aquarium.

SpaceFace becomes special when: **the miner, pirate, cargo hauler, asteroid, patrol ship and
gravity field are all part of the same physical situation.**

## The Massline is a signature mechanic, not a gadget

The Massline should become one of those mechanics people identify with the game immediately.

It is not just a grappling hook. It temporarily changes the relationship between two physical
bodies. That lets the player:

- swing around large objects;
- redirect their own momentum;
- tow things;
- snare enemies;
- turn enemies into moving hazards;
- steal or recover cargo;
- use heavy bodies as temporary anchors;
- create slingshot routes through the world.

The important part is that the game does not perform the cool move for the player. The player
supplies intent. The physical rules supply the consequence.

A good Massline moment should look complicated afterward while having required only a few
understandable actions to create. That principle applies to the whole game:

> **Complex-looking outcomes from simple, trustworthy rules. Not complex controls for their own
> sake.**

## Combat should feel delightfully abusive

SpaceFace is not trying to produce symmetrical honorable dogfights. The player should eventually
become extremely physically dangerous.

**Light enemies are almost ammunition.** They can be:

- shoved;
- spun;
- chained;
- clustered;
- slammed into terrain;
- thrown into other enemies;
- dragged into dangerous fields;
- scattered through cargo traffic.

Medium enemies require more commitment. Heavy enemies become moving terrain or physical problems.
Specialists disrupt what the player is trying to do.

The challenge comes from:

- positioning;
- numbers;
- collateral;
- specialists;
- dangerous geometry;
- commitment;
- law;
- protecting something;
- getting yourself into a terrible trajectory.

Not from simply making every enemy absorb thirty seconds of damage.

The player should grin because the game lets them become unfair in ways they earned.

## The world is not scenery

A mining station should not look alive because twenty ships randomly orbit it. It should look alive
because:

> a miner reaches an asteroid seam → extracts material → cargo accumulates → a hauler arrives →
> cargo changes hands → the hauler departs along a route → pirates notice valuable traffic →
> patrols respond → **the player can intervene anywhere in that chain.**

That chain is more valuable than fifty decorative NPCs.

Every active area should give the impression: *this place has a job. These people are doing it. I
just arrived in the middle of their day.*

Then the player is free to become: the helpful stranger, the opportunistic thief, the reckless
idiot, the protector, the saboteur, or the reason everyone now needs insurance.

## Failure should make better stories

SpaceFace should not constantly answer mistakes with "MISSION FAILED — RELOAD?". Failure should
usually mutate the situation.

- The convoy died? Now there is salvage.
- You accidentally destroyed someone's cargo? Now they want restitution.
- Your heist went loud? Now the heist has become an escape.
- Your ship was disabled? Now you have a recovery problem.
- A pirate escaped? Maybe they show up later.
- A faction lost a facility? Its traffic changes.
- A rare shipment vanished? Prices move.

The player should regularly tell stories about SpaceFace in the language of events:

> "I was supposed to steal the cargo, but I accidentally knocked the escort into it, which
> destroyed half the shipment, then the cops showed up, so I rescued the pilot I had nearly killed
> and escaped using the planet."

That is far more valuable than "I completed Mission 17."

## Progression means increasing physical agency

Progression should make the player think **"What can I do now?"** — not "my damage number went
from 170 to 212."

A new fitting might let the player:

- move much larger objects;
- survive high-speed collisions;
- produce enormous impulse;
- sustain stronger Massline loads;
- deploy gravity fields;
- carry stolen cargo without becoming immobile;
- operate in dangerous regions;
- become an unusually good anchor;
- perform a new type of physical heist.

The ship itself should become a long-lived personal machine. It gets scars. It gets weird fittings.
People recognize it. The player learns exactly how it handles. Eventually the ship should feel less
like an item in an inventory and more like:

> **"This is my fucking ship."**

## The visual fantasy

SpaceFace should not look like muted brown-grey hard sci-fi. It should look like **industrial
machinery pushed to arcade levels of energy**.

Space is dark because darkness gives the action somewhere to explode. Against it:

- bright engines;
- white-hot Masslines;
- long velocity trails;
- huge directional impacts;
- colorful cargo operations;
- mining beams;
- spinning machinery;
- gravity fields bending debris;
- burning wrecks;
- patched stations;
- traffic;
- painted hulls;
- warning lights;
- catastrophic flashes.

The world can be messy. The player's understanding cannot be. When something spectacular happens,
the player should still understand: what caused it, what moved, and why.

## What SpaceFace must never collapse into

Do not accidentally turn it into:

- a normal space shooter with a grappling hook;
- an HP-bar dogfighting game;
- an X4-style empire manager;
- a crafting treadmill;
- a loot-rarity ARPG;
- a procedural galaxy whose locations all play the same;
- a dialogue-heavy RPG;
- a realistic orbital simulator;
- a collection of disconnected minigames;
- a particle-effects showcase where the underlying event was boring.

Whenever designing something, ask:

1. Does this give the personally piloted ship another interesting physical decision?
2. Does it put meaningful bodies, relationships, opportunities, or consequences into the player's
   immediate world?
3. Can something surprising emerge from combining it with systems that already exist?

If the answer is no, it probably does not belong near the center of SpaceFace.

## The 60-second fantasy

Imagine someone sees SpaceFace for the first time.

A brightly painted mining operation is working around several asteroids. Cargo pods are being
loaded. A freighter leaves. Pirates intercept it. The player boosts into view. One pirate takes an
impulse hit and spins violently off course. The player Masslines another, swings around a huge
asteroid, builds speed, and releases. The pirate becomes a projectile. It tears through another
ship. Fragments scatter into a gravity field and curve visibly inward. Loose cargo spills
everywhere. The civilian freighter starts fleeing. A patrol enters from offscreen because somebody
witnessed the disaster. The player grabs one valuable cargo pod with the Massline and accelerates
away while WANTED.

All of that came from a few simple physical verbs operating inside a world that was already doing
something.

**If SpaceFace can make moments like that happen naturally and repeatedly — that is the game.**

# Part II — What this game is supposed to feel like (the owner, 2026-09-03)

**Status: PRODUCT AUTHORITY, same rank as Part I.** The owner's words, lightly paragraphed; nothing
here has been reworded. Where Part I and Part II say the same thing, Part II says it more precisely.
When a packet's "done when" cannot be traced to a sentence in this file, the packet is content, not
convergence.

SpaceFace is supposed to feel like being dropped into a tiny, busy, dangerous mechanical universe and
being told: **everything here is real enough to fuck with.**

Not real in the sense of astrophysical simulation. Not real because every bolt has a spreadsheet
behind it. Real because things have weight, direction, purpose, consequence, history, and
relationships to one another.

A freighter is not scenery. It is carrying something somewhere. The cargo exists because someone
produced it. Someone wants it. Someone may be protecting it. Someone else may be waiting to steal it.

A damaged ship is not merely an entity with low health. It is now a problem. Maybe it needs a tender.
Maybe it starts drifting. Maybe its cargo comes loose. Maybe a scavenger notices. Maybe the patrol
that was protecting it has to choose between chasing the attacker and staying with the wreck. Maybe
the player caused all of this accidentally. That is the game.

SpaceFace should constantly feel as though the player has arrived halfway through a story that nobody
wrote specifically for them. The world was already moving. You simply entered it. And then you
touched something.

## The fantasy

The fantasy is not: *I am the chosen hero of a space opera.* It is not: *I own a galactic empire and
watch numbers increase.* It is not: *I fly circles around enemies while holding the fire button until
their health disappears.* It is not even primarily: *I am a spaceship pilot.*

The deeper fantasy is:

> **I understand how this little universe moves, and because I understand it, I can make absurd
> things happen.**

You learn the weight of your ship. You learn how it carries momentum. You learn what a heavy ship can
shrug off and what a light ship can be turned into. You learn when to boost, when to drift, when to
cut thrust, when to let velocity do the work.

You learn that an asteroid is not background scenery. It is cover. It is an anchor. It is a weapon. It
is a place to sling around. It is something an enemy can be thrown into. It is something you can
accidentally hit at a horrifying speed.

The universe gradually stops looking like a collection of objects. It starts looking like a
collection of possibilities. That transformation in the player's perception is one of the most
important things SpaceFace can achieve.

A new player sees: asteroid, pirate, freighter, cargo pod. An experienced player sees: anchor,
projectile, momentum source, theft opportunity. That is mastery. Not bigger numbers. A richer
understanding of what the same world can do.

## The player should feel dangerous because they are clever

SpaceFace is not interested in honorable combat. It should be a little mean. A little funny. A little
irresponsible.

Eventually the player should become terrifying — not because the enemies have politely agreed to
stand still while a damage statistic rises, but because the player has learned how to abuse the
physical world.

A light enemy is not merely a target. A light enemy is almost ammunition. Push it. Spin it. Tether it.
Drag it. Throw it. Use it as cover. Use it as a flail. Send it into another enemy. Send both of them
through loose cargo. Pull several ships together and put something explosive in the middle. Knock a
pursuer off its attack line and watch it discover an asteroid at several hundred meters per second.

The funniest and most satisfying SpaceFace encounters should often look completely unreasonable after
they happen. The player should occasionally sit there looking at the wreckage thinking: *Holy shit. I
did that.* And sometimes: *Oh fuck. I did that.* Both are success. Because the second one means the
game respected the action enough to let it have consequences.

## The Massline is not a grappling hook

The Massline is the purest expression of the game. It does not simply let the player pull an object
closer. It temporarily changes the relationship between two physical bodies. That sounds simple. It
should remain simple. But the consequences should be enormous.

Attach to a massive body and your own movement changes. Attach to something lighter and its movement
changes. Attach while both bodies are moving and now you have a temporary coupled system with
tension, momentum, angle, mass, thrust, and timing.

From that one understandable relationship come: towing; stealing; rescue; slingshots; orbiting;
hitchhiking; cargo handling; enemy displacement; improvised weapons; desperate escapes; stupid
accidents; beautiful maneuvers.

The player should not need fifteen buttons to achieve this. The complexity should happen after the
input. A few understandable actions should create an outcome that looks almost choreographed. That is
a central SpaceFace principle:

> **Simple rules. Complicated consequences.** Never reverse that.

Do not make the controls complicated so that the result can be simple. Do not make the computer
secretly perform the interesting maneuver. The player provides intention. The rules provide
consequence. Assistance should remove meaningless keyboard precision when intent is obvious. It should
not steal authorship.

## Two speeds, and they must coexist

1. **Nimble in a fight.** Zip around, stay in control of the combat area, turn NOW when I twitch, stop
   when I brake, drift when I choose to. Response starts instantly. The ship feels like a
   controllable mass, not a cursor.
2. **Fast across the galaxy.** If I swing well, slingshot well, fly well, I EARN speed and I KEEP it.
   Thrusters have a cap; physics-earned speed does not get eaten by the brakes. Speed is something
   skill builds.

The draw-path mode is the same promise: I sketch a trick move with the mouse, the ship RIPS through it
fast while the guns stay on the locked enemy so I can focus on flight. It smooths out my hand-jiggle;
it does NOT crawl through turns like a tram trying to stay perfectly on a line. It flies like a
skilled pilot would fly my sketch — fast, controlled, cutting where it should, slowing only where a
real pilot must. If it follows the line at walking speed, it failed, even if it stayed perfectly on it.

## The world must be busy for a reason

A common failure in games is to make a place look alive by sprinkling NPCs through it. That is not
enough here. Twenty ships orbiting randomly around a station is not a living station. It is a
screensaver.

A place becomes alive when the player can understand what is happening. A mining operation should have
a logic. A miner reaches a seam. The machinery activates. Ore is produced. Material accumulates. A
hauler arrives. Cargo changes hands. The hauler departs. A survey ship searches for the next productive
area. A tender services damaged machinery. A patrol occasionally passes through. A pirate watches the
route because valuable cargo repeatedly leaves it. A scavenger appears after something goes wrong.

Now the place is alive. Because every actor has a sentence: **I am here because ______.** And
another: **If something changes, I will ______.** That is far more important than raw NPC count.

The player should arrive somewhere and immediately feel: these people have jobs. This place has a
function. Something was happening before I got here. Something will continue happening if I leave.
And most importantly: **I can interfere with it.**

## The player is not the center of the universe

SpaceFace should not constantly wait for the player to press ACCEPT MISSION before anything
interesting happens. A freighter can be attacked whether the player cares or not. A mining operation
can have an accident. A pirate can escape. A patrol can intercept contraband. A ship can break down. A
salvage crew can discover a wreck. A construction site can progress. A convoy can pass through. A
station can become damaged. Prices can move because something happened somewhere else.

The player may never see some of these events directly. That is fine. But important things should
leave evidence: wreckage; missing traffic; changed prices; a patched structure; a new warning; a named
pilot who remembers; a rumor; a damaged route; a salvage opportunity.

The universe should feel capable of continuing without the player. That is what makes interfering
with it meaningful. You cannot disrupt a world that exists only when you look at it.

## Failure should create content

The worst outcome for many interesting situations is: MISSION FAILED. RELOAD? SpaceFace should resist
that. Failure should often transform the problem.

The convoy died. Now there is wreckage. The valuable cargo survived. Now everyone wants it. You
accidentally killed the pirate you were supposed to interrogate. Maybe the wreck contains something
useful. Your heist went loud. The heist is now an escape. Your ship got disabled. Now rescue, repair,
debt, impound, towing, or desperate improvisation become the game. A target escaped. Maybe that person
exists later. You destroyed someone's cargo. Now there may be restitution, hostility, insurance
consequences, a criminal record, or simply someone who hates you. A facility was destroyed. Traffic
changes. Demand changes. Someone has to repair it. Someone profits from the shortage.

Failure should generate new verbs, not merely erase progress. SpaceFace stories should naturally
contain the words "So then…". The best anecdote structure is: I was doing X. Then Y happened. So I
tried Z. Which caused something worse. And somehow I escaped. That is better than a perfectly executed
script.

## Combat should be physical before it is numerical

Damage matters. Weapons matter. Loadouts matter. But if combat can be reduced to *face enemy, hold
fire, reduce HP, repeat*, then SpaceFace has failed to use its own identity.

Position should matter. Velocity should matter. Mass should matter. Terrain should matter. Cargo
should matter. Who is nearby should matter. Law should matter. Collateral should matter. The direction
of an impact should matter.

Enemies should create different physical problems. A fast light interceptor should not merely be "low
HP fast enemy." Its behavior should create a positioning problem. A heavy vessel should not simply
have more health. Its mass should change what the player can physically do to it. A specialist should
threaten the player's plan. Maybe it cuts tethers. Maybe it disrupts fields. Maybe it anchors allies.
Maybe it protects cargo. Maybe it forces the player to change trajectory. The roster should change the
shape of play, not merely the arithmetic.

SpaceFace should reward the player for looking at a situation and thinking: *What can I do with all of
this?* Not simply: *Which gun has the highest DPS?*

## The game should be fast

There is a temptation, because SpaceFace has economies and factions and industry and persistent
systems, to make it feel stately. Do not. The universe can be deep. The player's hands should feel
alive.

Ships should move with confidence. Boost should feel violent. Exceptional speed should open the camera
and stretch the world. A Massline release should feel like something snapped loose. Impacts should
answer instantly. Weapons should have distinct force. Small enemies should often resolve quickly.

The game can have thoughtful systems without requiring the moment-to-moment action to become slow.
Think: **deep world, arcade hands.** The player can spend ten minutes thinking about where cargo came
from and then spend three seconds sending a pirate into industrial machinery. Those are not
contradictory. That contrast is part of the appeal.

## Colorful without becoming cartoonish

SpaceFace should look like industrial machinery that has been pushed into an arcade game. Not toy
plastic. Not children's animation. Not generic gray science fiction. Not tasteful dark navy with
restrained cyan accents. Not "premium sci-fi UI kit #47."

Space itself should remain dark because darkness makes everything else explosive. Against that
darkness: bright engines; painted hulls; warning lamps; hot machinery; work lights; mining beams;
cargo illumination; Masslines; force fields; velocity streaks; debris; sparks; fires; gravity
distortions; violent impacts; glowing salvage; catastrophic flashes.

The world geometry can be dirty and worn and believable. The action should be luminous. A still
screenshot should communicate function and identity. A moving scene should communicate energy. The
graphics should become more impressive when the game starts moving, not less. Motion is part of the
art direction. The camera is part of the art direction. The aftermath is part of the art direction.

A high-speed sling should change the composition of the entire screen. A massive collision should
temporarily dominate attention. A distant mining site should read as activity through rhythmic light
and motion.

Effects should have shape and direction: cones, sheets, rings, ribbons, arcs, trails, sprays. Not
simply glowing spheres placed on events. The player should be able to understand the cause of a
spectacular event while being impressed by it. **Spectacle without causality is noise. Causality
without spectacle is a debug build.** SpaceFace needs both.

## Everything important should have a silhouette

Top-down perspective is not a limitation. It is a gift. The player can see relationships: trajectory,
formation, geometry, approaching threats, cargo, routes. Because of that, things must read quickly.

A miner should look like something that mines. A tanker should look like something carrying something
dangerous. A tug should look capable of pushing or pulling. A customs ship should look official before
a label appears. A scavenger should look adapted to handling wreckage. A heavy enemy should
communicate mass. A fast enemy should communicate direction. A station should communicate what
happens there.

Do not solve identity with floating text. Do not solve identity with recoloring the same hull. Do not
solve identity by adding tiny details the normal camera cannot see. Design from the gameplay camera
backward: silhouette first, motion second, functional equipment third, material language fourth,
details afterward.

## Industry should eventually become player authorship

Mining should not exist because games are expected to contain resource gathering. Industry should not
exist merely as passive income. The deeper purpose is transformation.

At first the player extracts. Then they move material. Then they understand routes. Then they acquire
better tools. Then they create infrastructure. Then that infrastructure creates new traffic. New
traffic creates new opportunities. New infrastructure changes how places function.

Eventually the player should be able to look at a region and recognize their own history in it. A
depot exists because they built it. A route is busy because their operation needs it. A station has
expanded. A faction depends on something they created. A scarred asteroid has been worked for hours.
A wreck still exists because of a battle they caused. The world becomes increasingly authored by the
player's actions. That is a much more powerful fantasy than "Factory income: +430 credits/minute."
Numbers may measure the world. They should not replace it.

## Progression should mean "what can I do now?"

A good upgrade makes the player imagine possibilities. Not merely compare percentages. Maybe now the
player can move a much heavier object. Maybe they can survive momentum that would have destroyed them
earlier. Maybe the Massline can sustain a more extreme load. Maybe their ship can act as an anchor.
Maybe they can deploy a gravity field. Maybe they can carry absurd cargo. Maybe they can operate close
to dangerous planetary conditions. Maybe they can perform a heist that was physically impossible
before.

The player should periodically look at a new capability and immediately think: *Wait. Could I use this
to…?* That is progression. The progression system should feed curiosity.

Eventually the player's ship should become deeply personal. Not because of a character creator.
Because of accumulated history. Damage. Repairs. Strange fittings. Behavior the player has learned
intimately. Maybe people recognize it. Maybe parts of the world react to its reputation. The ship
should become *my fucking ship* rather than *ship_tier_07*.

## The game needs ordinary life

Not every moment should explode. A living world needs normality so disruption has meaning. Ships doing
boring jobs. Routine inspections. Cargo transfers. Repair work. Workers waiting. A tug slowly
repositioning something enormous. A survey ship methodically scanning a field. Passenger traffic.
Construction. A patrol cruising through a safe lane. A quiet wreck nobody has touched yet.

These moments matter. They give the world texture. They establish what "normal" looks like. Then when
something goes wrong, the change is legible. Without ordinary life, every event becomes noise.
Without danger, ordinary life becomes wallpaper. The rhythm matters: work, travel, curiosity,
opportunity, tension, violence, aftermath, quiet, new opportunity. The game should breathe.

## Not everything needs to be systemically important

SpaceFace should be deeply systemic. But that does not mean every piece of content needs to connect
seven systems. Some things can simply be memorable. A bizarre abandoned tug spinning slowly beside a
refinery. A courier flying far too fast through traffic. A tiny shrine welded onto an industrial
structure. A pirate ship with a ridiculous improvised ram. A civilian vessel that keeps a ridiculous
distance from anything dangerous. A salvage crew arguing over a wreck. A field of old cargo pods from
an accident decades ago. A famous ship passing through. A strange signal. A beautiful quiet route. A
very large tanker whose entire purpose is to make everything else feel small.

Games need texture. Not every interesting thing must justify itself through a dependency graph.
Sometimes its job is to make the universe feel specific.

## Humor without becoming a comedy

Physics produces comedy naturally. Respect that. A ship pinwheeling through cargo. A terrible maneuver
that somehow works. An enemy accidentally solving another problem for you. A small craft being dragged
somewhere it very much does not want to go. The player attaching themselves to something enormously
more powerful and immediately regretting it.

The humor should usually emerge from physical truth. Not constant joke dialogue. The universe can take
itself seriously while the physics occasionally refuses to cooperate with anyone's dignity. That
contrast is wonderful.

## Story should live inside the world

SpaceFace can have characters, factions, motives, mysteries, politics, history. But story should not
turn the game into a conversation tree with a space game attached.

A character matters because what they want changes something real. A faction matters because its
priorities shape routes, patrols, prices, construction, conflict and opportunity. A mystery matters
because it draws the player somewhere strange and changes what they understand or can do. A betrayal
matters because a relationship in the world changes.

Narrative should put pressure on systems. Systems should create unexpected narrative. The authored
story provides motives. The simulation provides consequences. Neither should completely control the
other. The result should sometimes surprise even the designer.

## Every place needs a reason to exist

Sectors should not be palette swaps. Stations should not be menu containers floating in different
backgrounds. Planets should not simply be giant decorative spheres. Every major place needs a verb and
a rhythm.

What happens here? What moves through here? Why do people come here? What makes it dangerous? What
makes it profitable? What does law look like here? What does crime look like here? What kinds of ships
belong here? What kinds of structures exist because of local conditions? What can the player
physically do here that feels different? What happens when the place is damaged? What remains when
something goes wrong?

A player should eventually be able to recognize a sector from a screenshot without reading its name.
Better still: from thirty seconds of activity. Ceres should feel like Ceres because of what it does.
Helios should feel different because its economy, traffic, law, geometry, materials, cadence and
opportunities are different. A sector identity is not a color grade. It is a way of life.

## Denser without becoming cluttered

Professional games cheat density. SpaceFace should too. The player does not need every distant worker
to consume full simulation. There should be layers. Foreground activity is real and interruptible.
Midground activity is cheaper but coherent. Background activity implies a larger world through moving
lights, distant ships, machinery, cargo movement, mining flashes, traffic crossings, construction
activity.

The important rule is: **the closer the player gets, the more truthful the world becomes.** Do not put
fake interactable things in front of the player. But do not waste full simulation on everything beyond
attention range. The universe should look larger than the simulation budget while remaining honest
where interaction becomes possible.

## The game must not become a warehouse of features

SpaceFace already has enough ideas to become terrible if every idea is implemented separately. The
goal is not more menus, more catalogs, more modes, more special-case buttons, more systems that only
talk to themselves. The goal is combinations.

Movement becomes traversal. Traversal becomes combat. Combat produces aftermath. Aftermath becomes
salvage. Salvage affects economy. Economy creates traffic. Traffic creates crime. Crime creates law.
Industry creates infrastructure. Infrastructure changes traversal. The changed world produces new
physical situations.

That loop is what makes the game feel larger than its raw feature count. Whenever possible: **do not
add another feature. Make existing features collide.**

## What an A-list version of SpaceFace looks like

Imagine someone watching the game for sixty seconds with no explanation.

They see a colorful industrial pocket surrounded by deep space. Miners are visibly working. Machinery
moves. Cargo is accumulating. A hauler arrives and physically receives it. A fast civilian ship
crosses through. A patrol passes. Something huge moves slowly in the background.

The player comes screaming into frame. A pirate intercepts the hauler. The player boosts toward them.
The camera opens slightly with speed. A weapon impact hits one pirate sideways. The ship spins
violently. Its engine trail corkscrews behind it. The player Masslines another ship and swings around
a large asteroid. For one instant the screen becomes a diagram of tension and motion. Then the player
releases. The enemy becomes a projectile. It hits another ship. That ship clips a cargo pod. Cargo
spills. The first pirate crashes into industrial equipment. A gravity field bends loose wreckage
inward. The civilian hauler panics and accelerates away. A patrol notices the violence. The player is
now wanted.

Instead of continuing the fight, the player grabs a valuable cargo pod with the Massline. They boost
away. They almost hit an asteroid. They barely correct. They sling around the refinery structure and
disappear at ridiculous speed.

Behind them: debris; a damaged ship; a stranded pilot; loose cargo; a patrol pursuit; a disrupted
industrial operation; a salvage opportunity; a changed local situation.

Nobody scripted that exact story. But every part of the game was designed so that stories like it were
possible. That is the target. Not merely the visual spectacle. Not merely the physics. Not merely the
economy. The fact that all of them became the same event.

## The standard for every developer

Whenever you build something for SpaceFace, ask:

- Does this put something meaningful into the player's immediate world?
- Can the player physically interact with it?
- Does it have a reason to exist independent of the player?
- Can something else respond to what happens to it?
- Does failure create another interesting state?
- Does it look unmistakable at the actual gameplay camera?
- Does it move or behave in a way that communicates what it is?
- Does it create a new possibility rather than simply another statistic?
- Can it combine with systems that already exist?
- Does it make the game more specifically SpaceFace?
- Would somebody voluntarily interact with it if there were no mission marker telling them to?
- Could something surprising happen?
- Could the player tell a story about it afterward?

And finally: **does this make the universe feel more like a real box of dangerous toys?**

Because that is what SpaceFace ultimately is. A box of dangerous toys with consequences. A universe of
moving machinery, working people, criminal opportunities, momentum, light, cargo, scars, accidents,
greed, industry and ridiculous physical improvisation.

It should feel deep without feeling slow. Chaotic without becoming unreadable. Colorful without
becoming childish. Systemic without becoming sterile. Funny without becoming a joke. Physical without
becoming a simulator. Persistent without becoming an empire-management spreadsheet.

It should give the player freedom without removing consequences. Power without removing danger.
Complex outcomes without complex controls. A living world without turning the player into a
spectator.

And above everything else: **the player should feel that the universe is alive enough to surprise
them, solid enough to understand, and permissive enough to abuse.** That is SpaceFace.
