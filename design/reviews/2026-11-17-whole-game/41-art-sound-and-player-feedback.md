# What a pilot can see, hear and remember

**Art direction.** A heavy object hanging from a fine bright line gives SpaceFace an image of its own. Build the art around the seconds before and after the cut. Make the tug, payload and intended victim legible at a glance. The rest of the frame can then afford to be extravagant. Put the refinery lights behind the delivery collar, the welders on the scaffold, the freight queue beside the gate. A place starts to feel expensive when the player can say what happens there.

## The visual evidence

I examined the five actual store stills at thumbnail scale and the checked-in November 11 four-sector comparison. The September cut shot puts visible energy on both ends of the line. The swing shot makes a clean diagonal but its far end is an object a prospective buyer has no reason to care about. The solo thrust shot spends most of its frame on black sky. The outpost shot contains enough industry to sell a place, yet a small view makes it difficult to pick out the player or the transaction. The salvage shot shows another crew already at work, the strongest ordinary-life premise in this set. Reframe it around a decision the pilot can interrupt. These judgments apply to the specific retained images inspected. ## Let physics score the scene

In the public Foundry first fight, a right-click produced a visible long line. The HUD reported **1,690 t** and **55% strain**. After release, “MESSY · NEVER SWUNG” appeared. These three pieces describe a mechanic with depth, but only the grade explained my failure. At that moment a tiny tangential arrow or a rising strain cue *before* the cut would have taught me how to try again. Tie the correction to actual geometry. A bad release at speed might instead be a wrong target, an obstructed trajectory or a perfectly launched miss. The coach should diagnose only what its samples support. [Tether instrument](https://github.com/coldshalamov/SpaceFace/blob/4bbe545d550a4c43e720df18c254b1c8d190d8a1/src/audio/masslineInstrument.js) · [Core design](https://github.com/coldshalamov/SpaceFace/blob/4bbe545d550a4c43e720df18c254b1c8d190d8a1/design/game/18_SOUND_LOOK_AND_DONE.md)

The audio code already distinguishes six Massline events, makes pitch track load and differentiates release from break. A focused test verified the recipes, pitch and captions here. The priority bus defines a 250 ms, −8 dB duck on weapon and engine loops for a qualifying high-priority cue. That protects a narrow part of the mix. Music, ambience and comms can still compete perceptually. The mix assessment rests on source behavior and cue tests. Assign a listening pass before adding another voice bank. **A production-ready scene to commission.** In the Foundry, the floor goes briefly quiet as a freight sled crosses the pit. Its audible horn and lit path have already appeared at a safe distance. A Wasp crosses that path. The pilot can throw the Wasp into the sled, throw the sled at the pack, or miss and fly on. On success the sled continues trailing a damaged load, and the rhythm section comes back on impact. On a miss the horn passes and the opening repeats later. Use existing moving freight only if its collision owner accepts all three interactions. Otherwise stage the test with a throwable body already admitted by the arena. Have the first five testers describe what moved *after* the strike. The camera and score should help them notice their own act.

**Order of work.** First record public-route clips with the current render and audio owners. Second, fix the largest misunderstanding using one change to silhouette, lighting, motion or mix. Third, test the revised clip in motion and within the reference performance budget. Add art only for a documented confusion that the first changes leave unresolved. Keep an ungraded hard case in each batch, e.g. dark hull against furnace flare with bass-heavy music and captions active. A placid hangar screenshot cannot answer that question.
